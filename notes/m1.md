## business
### What is a business?
A **business** is an organization that tries to earn a **profit** by providing the **goods and services** its customers want.
- **Goods** are *tangible*: you can touch and store them (a laptop, a car).
- **Services** are *intangible*: they cannot be held, touched or stored (medical care, banking).

### What is an industry?
**Industry** refers to economic activities that **convert resources into useful goods**. It covers producing and processing goods and materials, and even breeding and raising animals. The word is also used for activities that use mechanical appliances and technical skill.

### Industry vs business
| Basis | Industry | Business |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A **group** of companies producing similar goods/services | An **individual** company doing commercial activity |
| Represents | A sector of the economy | One entity operating *inside* an industry |
| Example | Automotive industry, technology industry | Tesla, Apple |

> **Think of it like this:** the *industry* is the stadium, a *business* is one team playing in it.

### Four types of industry
1. **Primary**: extract raw materials directly from the earth or sea (mining, fishing, farming).
2. **Secondary**: process and **manufacture** products from raw materials (car plants, steel mills).
3. **Tertiary**: provide a **service** (banking, transport, retail).
4. **Quaternary**: use a high degree of **research and technology** and employ highly qualified people (biotechnology, computer programming).

### Why management matters for an engineer
Engineers rarely work alone. Projects need goals, teams, budgets and deadlines, which is exactly what management deals with. The course opens with a case asking you to choose which team to join: the point is that a well-*managed* team usually beats a talented but unmanaged one.

## functions
### Who is a manager?
Managers are individuals in an organization who **direct the activities of others**. An **organization** is *"a deliberate arrangement of people brought together to accomplish some specific purpose."*

A manager is responsible for **planning, organizing, leading and controlling** resources and activities to achieve goals, making sure the team works **efficiently** (no waste of resources) and **effectively** (reaching the goal).

### Three levels of management
| Level | Who | Typical focus (from the slides) |
|---|---|---|
| **Top** | CEO, directors | Executive coaching, change management, leadership, delegation & empowerment |
| **Middle** | Department heads | Problem solving, team building, talent development, performance management |
| **Lower / first-line** | Supervisors, foremen | Emotional intelligence and coaching for performance |

### Three managerial skills
- **Technical skill**: knowing *how to do the work* (methods, tools, processes).
- **Human skill**: working *with people*, motivating and communicating.
- **Conceptual skill**: seeing the *whole organization* and how its parts connect.

How they shift with level:
- **Top**: conceptual skill matters most.
- **Bottom**: technical skill matters most.
- **Human skill** is needed roughly equally at **every** level.

### The five functions of managers (POSLC)
1. **Planning**: selecting missions and objectives and the actions to achieve them. It needs **decision-making**, i.e. choosing a future course of action from alternatives.
2. **Organizing**: establishing an **intentional structure of roles** so every task needed for the goal is assigned to the people who can do it best.
3. **Staffing**: **filling and keeping filled** the positions in the structure. This covers identifying workforce needs, taking inventory of available people, then recruiting, selecting, placing, promoting, appraising, compensating and training them.
4. **Leading**: **influencing people** so they contribute to organizational and group goals. It is the interpersonal side of managing, so it involves **motivation, leadership styles and communication**. Leadership implies followership, and people follow those who help satisfy their needs.
5. **Controlling**: **measuring and correcting** performance so that events conform to plans. It shows where deviations from standards exist and helps correct them. Planning must come before controlling, because **plans are not self-achieving**.

> **Example:** opening a café. *Plan* the menu and target. *Organize* kitchen and counter roles. *Staff* by hiring a chef and baristas. *Lead* by motivating the team on day one. *Control* by checking daily sales against the target and fixing what's off.

## systems
### The organization as an open system
The **systems approach** sees an organization as a set of **interrelated, interdependent parts (sub-systems)** arranged so that the whole is *more than the sum of its parts*.

### How the system works
1. **Inputs** come from the external environment: people, capital, managerial skill, technical knowledge, energy, information, materials, finance.
2. The **transformation process** turns inputs into outputs *through the managerial functions* (plan, organize, staff, lead, control).
3. **Outputs** go back into the environment as goods, services, profits, satisfaction and goal integration.
4. **Communication** links every phase and connects the organization with its environment.
5. **Feedback / re-energizing**: some outputs (e.g. profit, satisfied employees) become **inputs again**.

### Key ideas
- **Sub-systems are related**: study parts in their inter-relationships, not in isolation.
- **Boundary**: separates the organization from other systems and decides what is internal or external.
- **Open system**: it interacts with and depends on its environment, so it is vulnerable to environmental change.
- **Dynamic**: its equilibrium keeps changing, so management must keep adjusting it.
- **Every system is part of a super-system** (a firm is part of an industry, which is part of the economy).
- **Multidisciplinary**: management draws knowledge from many disciplines.

> **Picture it:** a body. Food and air (inputs) are processed by organs working together (transformation), producing energy and activity (outputs). If one organ fails, the whole system suffers.

## fayol
### What is a principle?
A **principle** is a **fundamental truth** that explains cause and effect between variables in a given situation. **Management principles** are statements of fundamental truth based on logic that **guide managerial decisions and actions**. They come from (1) observing and analysing managers' practical experience and (2) experimental studies.

**Henri Fayol**, the *Father of Modern Management Theory*, gave a general theory that applies to all levels and departments.

### The 14 principles, explained
1. **Division of labour**: divide work and allot it by expertise. Specialization brings **speed, accuracy, efficiency and economy**.
2. **Authority & responsibility**: they **co-exist**. Authority is the right to get work done; responsibility is the obligation to perform. *Authority without responsibility leads to irresponsible behaviour; responsibility without authority makes a person ineffective.*
3. **Unity of command** (*one boss*): a subordinate takes orders from and is accountable to **only one superior**. Dual subordination undermines authority, weakens discipline, divides loyalty and causes confusion, delay, duplication and escaping of responsibility.
4. **Unity of direction** (*one head, one plan*): activities with the **same objective** are grouped under one manager with one plan. Unity of command is not possible without unity of direction.
5. **Equity**: fairness + kindness + justice. Managers must be fair and impartial, but equity does not mean no harshness: *"at times force and harshness might become necessary for the sake of equity."*
6. **Order**: systematic arrangement. **Material order** = a place for every thing; **social order** = the right person in the right job.
7. **Discipline**: sincerity, obedience, respect for authority and rules, required of **both** subordinates and management. It needs good superiors at all levels, clear and fair agreements, and judicious sanctions.
8. **Initiative**: eagerness to act **without being asked**. Encourage it with ideas schemes and monetary/non-monetary incentives; it builds trust and zeal.
9. **Fair remuneration**: pay should be fair and satisfy both sides. Base it on cost of living, work assigned, the firm's finances and prevailing wage rates. Fayol also suggested benefits such as free education, medical and housing facilities.
10. **Stability of tenure**: don't move employees around too often. People need time to learn a job; frequent transfers waste training effort. Stability builds team spirit and belonging.
11. **Scalar chain**: the chain of superiors from top to bottom through which all communication passes. In urgent cases a **gang plank** (a temporary direct link between two people at the same level) can be used, which shows principles are **flexible, not rigid**.
12. **Subordination of individual interest to general interest**: the organization's interest comes first; reconcile individual and group interests and, in conflict, the individual gives way. Needs honest employees, regular supervision and settling differences by agreement.
13. **Esprit de corps**: **team spirit** and harmony. Don't split workers into competing groups; encourage coordination, informal relations, enthusiasm, rewards for good work and face-to-face communication.
14. **Centralization & decentralization**: centralization keeps decision authority at the top; decentralization shares it downwards. *Anything that increases the subordinate's role is decentralization; anything that decreases it is centralization.* The right balance depends on business size, experience of superiors and ability of subordinates; neither extreme is feasible.

### Unity of command vs unity of direction
| Basis | Unity of command | Unity of direction |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | One subordinate, one boss | One head, one plan for activities with a common objective |
| Concerned with | Functioning of **personnel** | Functioning of **departments / whole organization** |
| Necessary for | Fixing each subordinate's responsibility | A sound organization |
| Avoids | Conflict, confusion, chaos | Duplication of effort, waste of resources |
| Result | Better superior–subordinate relations | Smooth running of the enterprise |

## roles
### Mintzberg's managerial roles approach
Henry Mintzberg (McGill University) studied what managers *actually do*. He found they play **ten roles** (organized sets of behaviour linked to a position) in **three categories**.

### A. Interpersonal roles (dealing with people)
1. **Figurehead**: ceremonial duties because of the position. *The president greets visiting dignitaries; a foreman attends a worker's wedding; the sales manager takes an important customer to lunch.*
2. **Leader**: responsible for the unit's people. Directly: hiring and training staff. Indirectly: motivating employees and reconciling their needs with organizational goals.
3. **Liaison**: building contacts **outside the vertical chain of command** (peers, outsiders) mainly to gather information.

### B. Informational roles (dealing with information)
4. **Monitor**: constantly **scans the environment** and contacts for information, much of it informal (gossip, hearsay, speculation).
5. **Disseminator**: **passes information inside** the organization to subordinates who would otherwise lack it.
6. **Spokesperson**: **sends information outside** the unit: advising directors and shareholders on financial performance, assuring consumer groups about social responsibility, satisfying government officials that the law is followed.

### C. Decisional roles (making decisions)
7. **Entrepreneur**: the **voluntary initiator of change**. Looks for new ideas and starts development projects, juggling several at a time.
8. **Disturbance handler**: **involuntarily responds** to pressures beyond control: a strike, a major customer going bankrupt, an accident delaying raw materials.
9. **Resource allocator**: decides **who gets what**, including the manager's own time; designs the unit's structure; authorizes important decisions.
10. **Negotiator**: spends considerable time in **negotiations**, a duty that cannot be avoided.

### How to identify a role in a case
Ask three questions in order:
1. Is the manager dealing mainly with **people**, **information** or a **decision**? That gives the category.
2. Is the action **inside** or **outside** the unit? (disseminator vs spokesperson)
3. Is change **chosen** or **forced**? (entrepreneur vs disturbance handler)

## csr
### The external environment
An organization is surrounded by forces it must respond to: **economic, technological, social, ecological, political & legal, and ethical** environments.

### Two views of social responsibility
- **Classical / purely economic view**: management's only social responsibility is to **maximize profits**.
- **Socio-economic view**: responsibility goes beyond profit to **protecting and improving society's welfare**.

Society expects managers to be responsible and ethical, yet financial scandals, scams over material quality and simple negligence show they don't always act that way.

### What is CSR?
**Corporate social responsibility** is a firm's intention, **beyond its legal and economic obligations**, to do the right things and act in ways good for society. It assumes the firm already obeys the law and pursues profit, and treats the business as a **moral agent** that must tell right from wrong.

The words: **Corporate** = organized business; **Social** = everything dealing with people; **Responsibility** = accountability between the two.

CSR is the corporate initiative to assess and take responsibility for the company's **effects on the environment and social welfare**, usually going **beyond what regulators require**. It is also called **"corporate citizenship"** and may mean accepting **short-term costs** with no immediate financial benefit in exchange for positive social and environmental change.

### Drivers of CSR
1. Employees and shareholders
2. **Brand equity** (the value of a brand as customers perceive its quality and desirability)
3. Ethical marketing practices and social awareness
4. Environmental consciousness
5. Energy conservation and global warming
6. Responsibility towards the government

### Why CSR pays off
- Satisfied employees become competent employees, giving better service and happier customers.
- Goodwill and trust take decades to build with customers and shareholders.
- An ethical culture reduces unfair practices by emphasizing professionalism and transparency.
- It shows up in socially responsible investment, communication, participation, fairness, adequate pay and personal development.

## ethics
### What is ethics?
**Ethics** is *"the discipline dealing with what is good and bad and with moral duty and obligation."* **Business ethics** systematically studies morals to separate actions that harm business from those that are ethically sound.

Ethics has often been dismissed as a "soft" topic because it was not linked to disciplines like economics and finance. The **integrated approach** connects ethics to everyday management decisions.

### Three moral theories (normative ethics)
| Theory | Core question | Slide examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Utilitarian** | Does it produce the **greatest good for the greatest number**? Judge by **consequences**. | Recycling programme for the whole community; affordable life-saving drug; shutting a polluting unit |
| **Rights** | Does it respect people's **basic rights** (conscience, free speech, due process)? | Not selling users' personal data; protecting journalists from censorship; paid leave to vote |
| **Justice** | Is it **fair, equitable and impartial**? | Equal pay for equal work; fair pricing for small vendors; scholarships for disadvantaged students |

> **Quick test:** *Consequences* → utilitarian. *Freedoms / entitlements* → rights. *Fair distribution* → justice.

### Institutionalizing ethics
Top managers must create an environment that encourages ethical decisions, in three ways:
1. A company **policy or code of ethics**
2. A formally appointed **ethics committee**
3. **Teaching ethics** in management development programmes

## trust
### Change management
**Change management** is a systematic approach to handling the transition or transformation of an organization's **goals, processes and technologies**. Its purpose is to implement strategies for bringing about and controlling change and to **help people adapt**.

### Why trust is the foundation
Managers face a flood of new concepts driven by global competition, customer expectations and the need to respond quickly. The often-overlooked factor is **trust**, which sits at the centre of **communication, collaboration and willingness to change**.

- Traditionally trust means **integrity, loyalty, caring and keeping promises** between individuals.
- It should go further: build a **culture of trust** across the organization that outlives any one leader, because *leaders come and go; the organization continues.*

> **Why it works:** people resist change when they fear hidden agendas. If they trust management, they will accept short-term discomfort for long-term benefit.

## international
### Culture shapes management
A country's culture influences employee behaviour and therefore management practice. The slides use **five dimensions**:
1. **Individualism vs collectivism**: do people look after themselves or their group?
2. **Large vs small power distance**: how much do people accept unequal power?
3. **Uncertainty tolerance vs avoidance**: how comfortable are people with risk and ambiguity?
4. **Masculinity vs femininity**: aggressive vs passive goal behaviour.
5. **Short-term vs long-term orientation**.

Managers must understand the cultural environment of the country they do business in.

### Country snapshots
- **Germany**: reliance on authority, often *benevolent authoritarianism*; managers show concern but expect obedience.
- **Austria (and Germany)**: self-realization and leadership; independence and competitiveness valued; **low** risk tolerance.
- **Australia**: moralistic stance, political and social values, achievement and **risk taking**.
- **Italy**: **low** risk tolerance; very competitive yet like **group decision-making**.
- **Britain**: job security, resourcefulness, adaptability, logic; **individualism** highly valued.

### Korea vs Japan vs USA
| Aspect | Korean | Japanese | American |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision-making | Top-down, hierarchical | Consensus-driven, participative | Top-down, fast, individual |
| Employment | Loyalty and seniority | Lifetime employment, loyalty | Performance-based, high mobility |
| Communication | Indirect, high-context | Indirect, high-context | Direct, low-context |
| Relationships | Hierarchy, respect for elders | Group harmony, team cohesion | Individualism, personal achievement |
| Leadership | Authoritative, paternalistic | Facilitative, consensus-oriented | Authoritative, results-oriented |
| Motivation | Loyalty, respect for authority | Group harmony, job security | Individual achievement, rewards |
| Conflict | Avoidance, saving face | Avoidance, harmony | Confrontational, quick resolution |
| Training | In-house, seniority-based | Extensive in-house, long-term | Formal education, continuous development |
| Innovation | Slow, cautious | Incremental, cautious | Rapid, proactive |
| Work-life | Long hours, dedication | Long hours, commitment | Work-life balance, flexible hours |
| Evaluation | Seniority and loyalty | Group performance and seniority | Individual results |
| CSR | Emerging | High, integrated | High, stakeholder/regulation driven |

> **Exam tip (Japan vs USA):** contrast them on 4–5 rows of this table (decisions, employment, communication, motivation, conflict) and link each to culture: collectivist & long-term (Japan) vs individualist & short-term (USA).

## theoryz
### Japanese vs American organizations (Ouchi)
| Feature | Type J (Japanese) | Type A (American) |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | Lifetime | Short-term |
| Decision-making | Collective | Individual |
| Responsibility | Collective | Individual |
| Evaluation & promotion | Slow | Rapid |
| Control | Implicit (understood) | Explicit (clear, precise) |
| Career path | Non-specialized | Specialized |
| Concern for employee | Holistic, as a person | Segmented, as an employee |

### Theory Z
**William Ouchi** developed Theory Z in the 1980s. It **integrates Japanese and American practices**: the Japanese emphasis on **collective decision-making** with the American emphasis on **individual responsibility**. It aims to raise **employee loyalty** by offering long-term jobs and caring for the employee's well-being on and off the job. Ouchi argued it promotes **stable employment, high productivity, and high morale and satisfaction**.

### Type Z organization (the blend)
1. **Long-term** employment
2. **Consensual, participative** decision-making
3. **Individual** responsibility
4. **Slow** evaluation and promotion
5. **Implicit, informal control with explicit, formalized measures**
6. **Moderately specialized** career path
7. **Holistic concern, including family**

> **Memory trick:** Z sits between J and A. For each row, take the Japanese answer *except* responsibility (American: individual), and make control and career path a mix of both.
