## organizing
### What is an organization?
An **organization** is a collection of people pursuing **defined objectives**. Organizing divides work among employees and aligns tasks toward the company's ultimate goal. It is the **second managerial function**: it coordinates employees' work, procures resources and combines the two in pursuit of goals.

### Why organizations matter
They bring resources together to achieve goals, produce goods and services **efficiently**, **facilitate innovation**, use modern manufacturing and information technology, **adapt to and influence** a changing environment, **create value** for owners, customers and employees, handle challenges of diversity and ethics, and **motivate and coordinate** employees.

### Nature and purpose of organizing
Organization implies a **formalized, intentional internal structure of roles and positions**. For a role to mean something to a person, it needs:
1. **Verifiable objectives** (part of planning)
2. A clear idea of the **major duties and activities**
3. An understood **area of discretion or authority**, so the person knows what they can do to reach goals

### The four steps in organizing
1. **Identify the activities** of the organization (all the work that must be done).
2. **Organize (group) the activities** into departments and jobs.
3. **Classify the authority**: decide who has authority over what.
4. **Build the relationship between authority and responsibility**, so everyone knows to whom they report and for what.

> **Example (a college fest):** list every task (stage, food, sponsorship, publicity) → group them into committees → appoint committee heads with clear powers → define who reports to the fest secretary and who answers for what.

### Syllabus extras often asked in papers: line, staff and delegation
*These appear in the chapter syllabus and in past papers but are only named, not explained, in the slides. Build answers from the organizing principles below.*
- **Line authority**: the direct superior–subordinate chain of command (the scalar chain). Line managers make and carry out decisions that achieve the main goals.
- **Staff authority**: advisory or support authority. Staff specialists (legal, HR, quality) **advise** line managers but don't command the operating chain.
- **Functional staff authority**: a specialist is given authority over a *specific process or policy* in other departments.
- **Line-and-staff structure**: the line chain runs the operations; staff units sit beside it to advise and support. Draw it as a vertical chain with side boxes linked by dotted lines.
- **Delegation**: giving a subordinate **authority** to achieve expected results. Steps to use as a scaffold: (1) decide the **expected results**, (2) **assign tasks**, (3) **delegate enough authority**, (4) hold the person **responsible/accountable** and review. Link it to Urwick's principles of **delegation** and **parity of authority and responsibility**.

## formal
### Formal organization
The **intentional structure of roles** in a formally organized enterprise: well-defined jobs, each with a measure of **authority and responsibility**. People achieve goals by following the norms the structure lays down; each person is responsible for their own performance.

### Informal organization
A **network of personal and social relationships** that is *not required* by the formal organization but **arises spontaneously** as people associate. It is built on likes, dislikes, feelings and emotions; friendship groups are an example. Nobody designs it and it follows no written rules.

> **Slide picture:** the same org chart overlaid with a golf club, a bowling team, morning-coffee regulars and weekend adventure-sports friends. A vice president and a divisional manager may be in the same informal group despite being far apart in the formal chart.

### Formal vs informal organization
| Formal | Informal |
|---|---|
| Designed and created by top management | Comes up on its own |
| Planned | Not planned; spontaneous |
| Authority and responsibility fixed and defined | Authority based on personal acceptance |
| Requires an office to function | Functions through people |
| Rigid, definite, written constitution | Flexible, no fixed written rules |

## span
### Span of control (span of management)
The **number of subordinates who report directly** to an executive or supervisor. It directly shapes the organization:
- **Flat structure**: **few levels**, **wide** spans.
- **Tall structure**: **many levels**, **narrow** spans.

The **optimal span** must be narrow enough to keep **control** over subordinates and wide enough to **avoid micromanaging** (interfering).

### Eight factors affecting the optimal span
1. **Subordinates' training**: well-trained people need less supervision (wider span).
2. **Delegation of authority**: clear delegation allows a wider span.
3. **Planning**: clear plans reduce the need for instructions.
4. **Rate of change**: fast change needs closer contact (narrower span).
5. **Communication techniques**: good techniques allow wider spans.
6. **Kind of activity**: routine work allows wider spans than complex work.
7. **Kind of organization**.
8. **Organization level**: higher levels usually have narrower spans.

The slides also note **proximity of subordinates**, job complexity, employee ability, similarity of jobs, managerial ability and technology.

### Wide vs narrow span
| | Wide span (many per manager) | Narrow span (few per manager) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pros** | Quick decisions, less bureaucracy, development of lower levels, easier coordination, lower cost, motivated managers | Personal contact with employees, tightly organized team, benefit of specialization |
| **Cons** | Manager under pressure, technology hurdles, chaos in large teams, too many viewpoints, issues overlooked | High managerial salaries, difficult communication, more bureaucracy |

> **Memory:** *Wide → flat → fast but stretched. Narrow → tall → close but costly.*

## urwick
### Who was Urwick?
**Lyndall Fownes Urwick** was a British management consultant who **integrated earlier ideas (such as Fayol's)** into a comprehensive theory of administration. He wrote *The Elements of Business Administration* (1943).

### The ten principles of organizing
1. **Unity of objectives**: the structure is effective if it helps individuals contribute to **enterprise objectives**. Without a common aim, departments set their own, possibly conflicting, goals.
2. **Organizational efficiency**: the structure should achieve objectives with the **minimum of unsought consequences or costs**.
3. **Specialization**: divide work among subordinates by **qualifications, abilities and skills**.
4. **Span of management**: there is a **limit** to how many people one person can manage effectively; the number depends on underlying variables.
5. **Scalar chain**: a chain of command flowing from top to bottom that **clarifies who reports to whom**.
6. **Unity of command**: every subordinate answers to **one boss** at a time.
7. **Delegation**: delegate authority **as far down as possible**, and enough for people to achieve the expected results.
8. **Parity of authority and responsibility**: responsibility **cannot be greater or less** than the authority delegated. Subordinates' responsibility to superiors is **absolute**, and superiors **cannot escape** responsibility for their subordinates' activities.
9. **Flexibility**: the structure must be **simple** and **adaptable** to changes in business, technology and procedures.
10. **Functional definition**: every position and department must have a clear definition of **results expected, activities to be undertaken and authority delegated**.

### How to apply them to a case (most-asked topic!)
For each case clue, ask which principle it **violates or supports**:
| Clue in the case | Principle |
|---|---|
| Departments chasing their own conflicting targets | Unity of objectives |
| Two managers giving one employee orders | Unity of command |
| A manager with 40 direct reports struggling | Span of management |
| Responsibility given but no power to act | Parity of authority & responsibility / delegation |
| Nobody knows what a role is supposed to deliver | Functional definition |
| Jobs given to people without the right skills | Specialization |
| Structure can't cope with new technology | Flexibility |
| Wasteful, costly structure | Organizational efficiency |
| Unclear reporting lines | Scalar chain |

> **Memory:** *"Goals, Efficiency, Expertise, Span, Chain; One boss, Delegate, Parity, Flex, Define."*

## departmentation
### What is departmentation?
Designing a structure has two steps: first **divide the whole work into jobs** so no important activity is left out; then **group similar (homogeneous) jobs** and decide how the groups relate. This grouping is **departmentation**: a logical division of work into **manageable units**.

### Eight patterns
1. Simple numbers 2. Time 3. Enterprise function 4. Territory/geography 5. Product 6. Process/equipment 7. Customer 8. Matrix

### 1. By simple numbers
Group people doing the **same duties** under one supervisor, purely by headcount (e.g. labour gangs). An age-old method falling into disuse because technology demands **specialized skills**, and specialist groups are more efficient than groups formed by number. Useful only at the **lowest level**.

### 2. By time
One of the **oldest** forms, used at lower levels: grouping activities by **shifts** (e.g. hospitals, production plants).
| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Service available 24×7 instead of one 8-hour shift | Night-shift supervision is difficult |
| Continuous processes aren't interrupted | Fatigue: hard to change the biological clock |
| Expensive equipment used more than 8 hours a day | Coordination and communication problems between shifts |
| Students can work night shifts | Overtime pay raises costs |

### 3. By enterprise function
The **most widely used** form: group activities by **function** (production, sales, finance, HR). Similar activities sit in one department under one chain of command, consistent with **specialization**. It eases **coordination** because one supervisor runs one major area.
| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale within departments | Slow response to environmental change |
| In-depth knowledge and skill development | Decisions pile up at the top (hierarchy overload) |
| Accomplishes functional goals | Poor horizontal coordination between departments |
| Best with one or a few products | Less innovation; restricted view of overall goals |

### 4. By territory / geography
Group activities by **region** when customers or operations are **geographically dispersed**. *Coca-Cola: a North American sector and an international sector covering the Pacific Rim, Europe, Africa and Latin America.*
| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Places responsibility at lower levels | Needs more people with general-manager ability |
| Emphasis on local markets and problems | Hard to keep economical central services |
| Better coordination within a region | Harder for top management to control |
| Economies of local operation | |
| Better face-to-face communication locally | |
| Measurable training ground for general managers | |

> **Exam favourite:** *"An MNC with a sales field network all over the country"* → **territorial** departmentation (regions → zones → areas), with these merits and limitations.

### 5. By process / equipment
Group activities around a **process or type of equipment** (e.g. a painting department, a welding shop, a machining section). It uses specialized equipment and skills efficiently.

### 6. By customer
Group activities by **common customers or customer types** (e.g. retail vs corporate banking). The assumption is that each customer group has **common problems and needs** best met by specialists.

### 7. By product / service
Group activities around **products or product lines**; also useful in service businesses. *A food company with frozen food, dairy and produce departments.* It focuses responsibility on each product's results.

## matrix
### 8. Matrix departmentation
A matrix structure **superimposes a horizontal set of divisional (project/product) reporting relationships onto a vertical functional structure**. Its essence is **combining functional and project/product departmentation** in the same structure.

### How it works
- Functional heads (engineering, finance, marketing) run the **vertical** lines.
- Project managers run **horizontal** lines across functions.
- A team member therefore has **two bosses**: a functional manager and a project manager.

### Merits and limitations
| Merits | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Pools experts from different functions on one project | Breaks **unity of command** (two bosses) |
| Flexible use of specialists across projects | Possible conflict and confusion over authority |
| Project focus with functional depth | Needs strong coordination and communication |

> **How to spot it in a case:** experts from different departments brought together for a **project** while still belonging to their home department → **matrix**.

### Identification practice
