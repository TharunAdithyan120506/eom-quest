## leadership
### What is leadership?
**Leadership** is the art or process of **influencing people** so that they strive **willingly and enthusiastically** toward group goals. Ideally people develop not just willingness to work but *"willingness to work with **zeal** and **confidence**."*
- **Zeal**: ardour, earnestness and intensity in work.
- **Confidence**: comes from experience and technical ability.

Leadership means **leading, influencing, commanding and guiding** people.

### Managers vs leaders
| Managers | Leaders |
|---|---|
| Focus on **things** | Focus on **people** |
| **Do things right** | **Do the right things** |
| Plan, organize, direct, control | Inspire, influence, motivate |
| Follow the rules | Build and shape entities |

### Four ingredients of leadership
1. Ability to **use power effectively and responsibly**.
2. Ability to **understand that people have different motivating forces** at different times and situations.
3. Ability to **inspire**.
4. Ability to **develop a climate** that arouses and responds to motivation.

### Qualities of an effective leader
Not arrogant · not miserly · as open as possible with subordinates · makes sure the team knows what is expected · promotes competence and inspires · sees things from others' point of view · puts the organization's needs above their own.

## maslow
### Motive, motivator, satisfaction, motivation
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Motive** | An **inner state** that energizes and directs behaviour toward goals; the driving force |
| **Motivator** | The **technique** used to motivate people (e.g. a bonus) |
| **Satisfaction** | The **contentment** felt when a want is satisfied |
| **Motivation** | The **drive and effort** to satisfy a want; the process of stimulating people to act |

> **Easy way:** *motive* is inside you, a *motivator* is what the manager offers, *motivation* is the resulting drive, *satisfaction* is how you feel once the want is met.

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs
Needs form a ladder; **as each level is substantially satisfied, the next becomes dominant**.
1. **Physiological**: basic survival needs.
2. **Safety**: security and protection.
3. **Social**: belonging, acceptance, friendship.
4. **Esteem**: respect, status, recognition.
5. **Self-actualization**: *"the drive to become what one is capable of becoming."*

**Lower-order needs** (physiological, safety, social) are satisfied **externally**; **higher-order needs** (esteem, self-actualization) are satisfied **internally**.

### What organizations do at each level
| Need | How organizations meet it |
|---|---|
| Self-actualization | Skill development, chance to be creative, promotions, full control over one's job |
| Esteem | Merit pay rises, recognition, challenging tasks, participation in decisions, advancement |
| Social | Chances to interact, be accepted and make friends: parties, picnics, trips, office sports teams |
| Safety | Safe working conditions, job security, fringe benefits (medical insurance, sick pay, pensions) |
| Physiological | Adequate salary, work breaks, safe working conditions |

> **Case method:** read what the employee complains about or wants, name the level, then suggest the matching organizational action. "*I feel isolated from my team*" → social. "*Nobody notices my work*" → esteem.

## herzberg
### Two-factor (motivation–hygiene) theory
Also called the **dual-factor theory**. Herzberg argued there are **two separate, mutually exclusive sets of factors**: one causes **job satisfaction**, the other **dissatisfaction**.

| Motivators (satisfiers) | Hygiene factors (dissatisfiers) |
|---|---|
| Performance and **achievement** | **Salary** |
| **Recognition** | **Working conditions** |
| Job status | Physical workspace |
| **Responsibility** | Relationship with colleagues |
| Opportunities for **advancement** | Relationship with supervisor |
| Personal growth | Quality of supervisor |
| **The work itself** | **Policies and rules** |
| *Improving these increases satisfaction* | *Improving these decreases dissatisfaction* |

Motivators relate to **job content**; hygiene factors relate to **job context**. Removing hygiene problems only makes people **"not dissatisfied"**; to truly motivate you must add motivators.

### The four combinations
| | Low motivation factors | High motivation factors |
|---|---|---|
| **High hygiene** | Few complaints but **not motivated** | **Ideal**: no complaints and motivated |
| **Low hygiene** | **Worst**: not motivated and complaining | Motivated but **have complaints** |

### Maslow vs Herzberg
Herzberg's **motivators** match Maslow's **upper levels** (esteem and self-actualization). His **hygiene factors** match the **lower levels** (physiological, safety, social/belonging).

## xy
### McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y
Two contrasting sets of **managerial assumptions** about people. Theory X stresses **supervision**; Theory Y stresses **rewards and recognition**.

### Theory X assumptions (traditional)
- The average employee **dislikes work** and avoids it where possible.
- So people must be **persuaded, compelled or threatened** with punishment.
- **Close supervision**; managers are dictatorial.
- Employees rank **job security** highest and have little ambition.
- They **dislike responsibility**, **resist change**, and need **formal direction**.

### Theory Y assumptions (based on research)
- Work can be as **natural** as rest or play.
- People can use **self-direction and self-control** when committed to objectives; threats aren't the only way.
- Rewarding, satisfying work brings **loyalty and commitment**.
- The average person can **accept and even seek responsibility**.
- People have **creativity, resourcefulness and innovative potential** that should be fully used.

### Comparison
| Basis | Theory X | Theory Y |
|---|---|---|
| Work | Disliked | Natural |
| Ambition | Little or none | High |
| Responsibility | Avoided | Accepted and sought |
| Leadership style | **Autocratic** | **Democratic** |
| Direction | Constant | Little needed |
| Control | Tight | Lenient |
| Authority | **Centralized** | **Decentralized** |
| Self-motivation | Absent | Present |
| Needs focused on | Physiological and security | Social, esteem, self-actualization |

> **Link it up:** Theory X ↔ Maslow's lower needs ↔ autocratic style. Theory Y ↔ higher needs ↔ democratic style.

## motivationtechniques
### Positive techniques
- **Praise** workers and give due credit.
- Take a **sincere interest** in subordinates as individuals.
- Promote **healthy competition**.
- Develop **pride** in the workplace.
- **Delegate** substantial responsibility.
- Fix **fair wages** and individual or group **incentives**.
- Set up a **suggestion system**.
- Provide opportunities for **growth and promotion**.

### Negative techniques
**Reprimanding** (scolding), **demotion**, **lay-offs**, **discharge**. *The proper balance of positive and negative techniques is the mark of a skilful manager.*

### Special motivational techniques
1. **Money**: economists and most managers rank it high; behavioural scientists rank it low.
2. **Positive reinforcement**: reward desired behaviour so it is repeated.
3. **Job enrichment**: build more challenge, responsibility and achievement into the job.
4. **Participation**: involve people in decisions that affect them.

## authoritystyles
### Three theories of leadership behaviour
1. Leadership based on the **use of authority**
2. **Likert's** four systems of managing
3. The **managerial grid**

### Styles based on the use of authority (Kurt Lewin, 1939)
**Leadership style** is the manner of providing direction, implementing plans and motivating people.

**1. Autocratic leader**
- **Commands and expects compliance**; leads by the ability to give or withhold rewards and punishment.
- Tells employees what to do and how, **without their advice**.
- Suitable when the leader **has all the information**, **time is short**, and employees are well motivated.
- Should be used **rarely**. *Examples: military, manufacturing, construction.*

**2. Democratic / participative leader**
- **Consults** subordinates and **encourages participation**, but **keeps the final decision**.
- Used when the leader has **part of the information** and employees have the rest.
- A sign of **strength, not weakness**: it makes employees part of the team and improves decisions.

**3. Free-rein (delegative) leader**
- Uses power **very little**; gives subordinates **high independence** to set their own goals and methods.
- The leader is **still responsible** for the decisions.
- Used when employees can **analyse the situation themselves** and know what to do.

| Style | Where the power sits |
|---|---|
| Autocratic | **With the leader** |
| Democratic | **Shared** |
| Free-rein | **With the employees** |

### Forces that influence which style to use
How much **time** is available · relationships based on **respect and trust** or not · **who has the information** · how well **employees are trained** and how well the leader knows the task · **internal conflicts** · **stress levels** · **type of task** (structured, unstructured, complicated, simple) · **laws or established procedures**.

> **Case method:** creative R&D team with expertise → democratic or free-rein. Emergency, unskilled staff, no time → autocratic. Always justify with the case's facts.

## likert
### Likert's four systems of managing
**Rensis Likert** and associates (University of Michigan) studied managers in **200 organizations over three decades** and proposed four systems, which can be read as leadership styles.

| | System 1: Exploitative-authoritative | System 2: Benevolent-authoritative | System 3: Consultative | System 4: Participative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trust in subordinates** | None | **Patronizing** (condescending) | Substantial but not complete | **Complete** |
| **Motivation** | Fear, threats, punishment, occasional rewards | Rewards plus some fear and punishment | Rewards, occasional punishment, some participation | Participation and involvement |
| **Communication** | **Downward** only | Some upward | Up and down | Up, down and lateral; act as a group |
| **Decisions** | Only at the **top**; imposed | Some delegation, close control | Broad policy at top, specific decisions lower down | **Throughout** the organization |
| **Responsibility** | Upper levels only | Managerial levels, not lower | Spread widely | Widespread at all levels |
| **Teamwork** | Very little | Very little | Fair amount | **High** |

**Likert's finding:** managers using **System 4** were the most successful leaders; their departments set and achieved goals better and were **more productive**.

> **Memory:** *E–B–C–P: Exploit → Be kind (but boss) → Consult → Participate.* Trust and participation rise from 1 to 4.

## grid
### The managerial grid (Blake & Mouton)
A **self-assessment tool** developed by **Robert Blake and Jane Mouton** in the 1960s to identify a manager's style.
- **Horizontal axis**: **concern for production** (1 = low, 9 = high).
- **Vertical axis**: **concern for people** (1 = low, 9 = high).
- That gives **81 possible positions**. Write coordinates as *(production, people)*.

### The five key styles
| Grid | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **1,1** | **Impoverished** | Minimum effort on both; managers have practically abandoned the job and just pass messages along |
| **1,9** | **Country club** | High concern for people, little for production; relaxed, friendly, happy, but no coordinated effort toward goals |
| **5,5** | **Middle of the road** | Medium concern for both; adequate but not outstanding morale and output; goals not set too high; benevolently autocratic |
| **9,1** | **Authority-compliance / autocratic task** | Only efficiency matters; work arranged so human elements interfere minimally; autocratic |
| **9,9** | **Team management** | Highest dedication to both; work done by committed people with a "common stake", trust and respect |

> **Trap:** the **first** number is **production**, the second is **people**. 1,9 = low production, high people = country club.

### Putting the three theories together
| Use of authority | Likert | Managerial grid |
|---|---|---|
| Autocratic | System 1, System 2 | 9,1 |
| Democratic | System 3, System 4 | 9,9 / 5,5 |
| Free-rein | | 1,1 / 1,9 tendencies |

## continuum
### Leadership as a continuum (Tannenbaum & Schmidt)
Developed by **Robert Tannenbaum and W. H. Schmidt**. Leadership is not a choice between just two styles (authoritarian or democratic) but a **range of styles** from **highly boss-centred** to **highly subordinate-centred**, varying with the **degree of freedom** the leader gives subordinates.

- One end: the **area of freedom for managers** is large (authoritarian).
- Middle: democratic.
- Other end: the **area of freedom for non-managers** is large (free-rein).

The right style depends on **the leader, the followers and the situation**. An **ellipse** around the model shows the influence of the **organizational environment** and **societal environment** on style.

> **Picture:** a slider. Slide left and the boss decides and announces; slide right and the team decides within limits.

## groups
### Committees
A **committee** is a group of persons to whom, **as a group, some matter is committed**. Also called a board, commission, task force, team, self-managing team or autonomous work group; the essential nature is the same.

**Functions and formality:** committees can perform planning, organizing, staffing, leading and controlling. Some have authority to **make recommendations** to a manager; some only **receive information** without recommending or deciding.

### Reasons for using committees
1. **Group deliberation and judgment**
2. **Dominant logic**
3. **Fear of too much authority in a single person**
4. **Representation of interested groups**
5. **Coordination** of departments, plans and policies
6. **Transmission and sharing of information**
7. **Consolidation of authority**
8. **Motivation through participation**

### Disadvantages and misuse
- **Costly**; compromise at the **least common denominator** rather than the best decision; **indecision**; **split responsibility**; a **few may impose their will** on the majority.
- Misuse: committees should **not replace a manager**, be used for research, for unimportant decisions, or for decisions beyond members' authority.

### Four stages of group development
1. **Forming**: members get to know each other.
2. **Storming**: they work out the objective and **conflict** arises.
3. **Norming**: the group agrees on **norms** and rules of behaviour.
4. **Performing**: the group gets down to the **task**.

### Groups
**Two or more people acting interdependently in a unified manner toward common goals.** Characteristics: **common goals**, **interaction and communication**, members take **roles**, groups are part of a **larger group**, and they develop **norms** (expected behaviour).

**Functions and advantages:** groups are powerful in changing **behaviour, attitudes and values** and disciplining members; used for decision-making, negotiating and bargaining; diverse members bring different perspectives; structure affects communication; participation in setting objectives builds **commitment**; they give **social satisfaction**, belonging and support; and they promote communication.

## teams
### What is a team?
*"A small number of people with **complementary skills** who are committed to a **common purpose**, set of performance goals and approach for which they hold themselves **mutually accountable**."*

Some teams recommend, some decide, some run operations. Some solve problems (e.g. **quality circles**); others do **cross-functional** work (design, marketing, finance, manufacturing, personnel).

### Group vs team vs committee
| | Committee | Group | Team |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essence | A **matter is committed** to it | People acting **interdependently** toward common goals | **Complementary skills**, **mutual accountability** |
| Accountability | Collective, can be split | Individual | **Mutual** |

### Team building
- Members must believe the purpose is **worthwhile, meaningful and urgent**.
- Select members for the **skills needed**: technical, problem-solving and decision-making, and human-relations skills.
- Set **rules of behaviour**: regular attendance, confidentiality, fact-based discussion, everyone contributing.
- Identify **goals and tasks early**.
- Encourage one another with **recognition, positive feedback and rewards**.

### Types of teams
- **Self-managing team**: members with the various skills needed to carry out a relatively **complete task**.
- **Virtual team**: members are **not in the same location**, may not report to the team manager, and may not even work for the same organization.

## communication
### Why communication matters
Communication is **the means by which people are linked together** in an organization to achieve common goals; without it, coordination and change are impossible.
- **Internally**, it **integrates** the managerial functions of planning, organizing, staffing, leading and controlling.
- **Externally**, it links the enterprise to its environment: customers' needs, suppliers, stockholders' claims, government regulations and community concerns.

### The communication process
1. **Sender** has a **thought**.
2. **Encodes** it into symbols both sides understand.
3. **Transmits** it through a channel (oral or written: memo, computer, phone, TV).
4. **Receiver** **receives** and **decodes** it; how well depends on knowledge, experience, trust and relationship with the sender.
5. **Understanding**: effective only if the receiver understands it **exactly as intended**.
6. **Feedback**: the final step confirming correct interpretation (verbal or non-verbal).

**Noise** can disrupt any stage: physical, physiological, technical, organizational, cultural, psychological and **semantic** (language/words).

### Nine types of communication
**By formality**
1. **Formal**: the officially created flow between positions. *Memos, intranet, meetings, conferences, one-on-ones, bulletin boards, letters, presentations, notice boards, emails from leaders.*
2. **Informal (grapevine)**: outside the official system. Patterns: **single strand** (each tells the next in sequence), **cluster** (people pass it to their cluster or keep it), **probability chain** (each randomly tells another).

**By direction**
3. **Downward**: higher to lower levels; common in authoritarian settings; instructions, speeches, letters, policy statements. Time-consuming; information is often lost or distorted on the way down.
4. **Upward**: subordinates to superiors; often **filtered** (bad news held back); typical of participative settings; needs a climate where people feel free to speak.
5. **Crosswise / horizontal**: same or similar level, different areas (managers of HR, marketing, sales).
6. **Diagonal**: different levels with **no direct reporting relationship**, used to speed things up (a floor manager and the sales team).

**By medium**
7. **Written**: records, references, legal defence, carefully planned, reaches large audiences, uniform policy; but mountains of paper, poor writing, no immediate feedback.
8. **Oral**: face-to-face or presentations; **speedy interchange and immediate feedback**; questions can be asked.
9. **Non-verbal**: facial expressions and gestures that reinforce or **contradict** the words.

### General vs managerial communication
General communication is everyday exchange of information; **managerial communication** is purposeful communication that integrates the managerial functions and links the organization to its environment to achieve goals.

## barriers
### Barriers to effective communication
1. **Lack of planning**
2. **Unclarified assumptions**
3. **Semantic distortion**, deliberate or accidental (*"We sell for less": less than what?*)
4. **Poorly expressed messages**
5. **Loss by transmission** and **poor retention**
6. **Poor listening and premature evaluation**
7. **Distrust, threat and fear**
8. **Insufficient time to adjust to change**
9. **Information overload**

Others: **selective perception**, **attitude influence**, **differences in status and power**, **too many organizational levels**.

### Towards effective communication
- Senders must be **clear in their own minds** about what they want to say.
- Encode and decode with **familiar symbols**.
- Don't plan communication in a vacuum; consider the **receivers' needs**.
- Tone of voice, choice of words and **congruence between what is said and how** affect the reaction.
- Communication is complete only when the message is **understood**.
- It is more than transmitting information; it also deals with **emotions**.

### Listening ("listening is the key to understanding")
Stop talking · put the talker at ease · show you want to listen · remove distractions · empathize · be patient · hold your temper · go easy on arguments · ask questions.

### Improving written communication
Simple, short, familiar words · personal pronouns (I, you) · illustrations and examples · short sentences and paragraphs · **active verbs** ("the manager plans") · no unnecessary words.

> **Exam approach:** name the barrier, quote the case line that shows it, then give the matching remedy.

## controlprocess
### What is controlling?
Controlling is **verifying whether everything happens in conformity with the plan**, instructions and principles, to **point out weaknesses and errors, rectify them and prevent recurrence**. It applies to **things, people and actions**.

It is the **measurement and correction of performance** to ensure enterprise objectives and plans are achieved. Planning and controlling are closely linked, and controlling is the job of **every manager**, from president to supervisor.

### Establishing standards
Plans are the yardsticks for controls, but managers can't watch everything, so they set **standards**: **criteria of performance** at selected points where measurement gives signals about progress. A standard is a **unit of measurement that serves as a reference point for evaluating results**. Goals, objectives, quotas and targets also act as standards. *Examples: sales quotas, budgets, job deadlines, market share, profit margins.*

**Types of standards:** **physical**, **technical**, **monetary**, **managerial** and **time** standards.

### The eight steps in controlling
1. **Establish standards**
2. **Actual performance** happens
3. **Measure** actual performance
4. **Compare** actual performance with standards
5. **Identify deviations**
6. **Analyse the causes** of deviations
7. **Program corrective action**
8. **Implement** corrections (and loop back)

> **Worked example (a phone factory):** standard = 1,000 units/day → actual production runs → count 920 units → compare with 1,000 → deviation of 80 → cause: a machine breakdown → plan: maintenance schedule and a backup line → implement, then monitor again.

## controltypes
### Three types of control (by timing)
| Type | When | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feedforward** | **Before** | Anticipates problems **before they arise** (on inputs) | Hiring extra staff and stocking up before a festival rush |
| **Concurrent** | **During** | Immediate feedback on how efficiently **inputs are becoming outputs** | A supervisor checking quality on the line in real time |
| **Feedback** | **After** | Information on **customers' reactions** to goods and services | Customer satisfaction surveys after a launch |

> **Exam approach:** quote the case line, then point to its timing word: *in anticipation / before* → feedforward; *while / during* → concurrent; *after / survey / results* → feedback.

## budgets
*This and the following control lessons are **self-study material explicitly included for the end-semester exam**.*

### Management control techniques
Three categories: **budgetary controls**, **non-budgetary controls** and **network (time-event) techniques**.

### Budgetary control
Finding out what is being done and **comparing actual results with the budget**, to approve achievements or remedy differences. Budget types listed: fixed, flexible, master, sales, production, materials, labour and capital expenditure budgets.

A **budget** is a **single-use plan** detailing the resources, usually financial, needed for a programme, project or activity; managers review the reports and act.

### Three families of budgets
| Family | Purpose | Types |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | Where cash will come from and how it will be used | **Cash** budget (all cash in/out by month, week or day), **capital expenditure** budget (plant, machinery, land), **balance-sheet** budget (forecast assets and liabilities) |
| **Operating** | Planned operations in money terms | **Sales/revenue** budget, **expense** budget, **profit** budget (revenue − expenses) |
| **Non-monetary** | Planned operations in non-financial terms | **Labour** budget (hours), **space** budget (square feet), **production** budget (units) |

### Dangers in budgeting
- **Over-budgeting**: spelling out minor expenses, taking away managers' freedom.
- **Overriding enterprise goals**: budget targets become more important than company objectives.
- **Hiding inefficiency**.
- **Causing inflexibility**, the greatest danger: decisions become rigid.

## flexiblebudget
### Variable (flexible) budgets
Because inflexibility is dangerous, attention has turned to **variable budgets**, designed to **vary with the volume of sales or another measure of output**.
- Some costs don't vary with volume (especially in the short run); others range from fully to slightly variable.
- A fixed budget works fine with good plans and forecasts, but a variable budget **forces study of the factors that turn workload into labour and expense needs**.
- Typical layout: expense items (material, labour, overheads, engineering, R&D, sales and distribution) in rows; columns for sales volumes (e.g. 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 thousand) showing how each cost changes; then total cost, profit and profit-to-sales %.

### Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)
Similar in purpose to good variable budgeting. Divide programmes into **packages** (goals, activities, resources) and calculate each package's cost **from zero** every budget period, instead of just adjusting last period's figures.

> **Contrast:** *traditional* = last year + changes; *variable* = changes with output; *zero-based* = justify everything from scratch.

## nonbudget
### Non-budgetary control devices
1. **Statistical data**: show **trends** so managers can extrapolate; present as averages to remove seasonal or accounting-period effects; easiest to understand as **charts**, and should allow comparison with a standard.
2. **Special reports and analysis**: for particular problem areas where routine reports are inadequate; money spent on targeted analysis can be better value than elaborate routine systems.
3. **Operational audit**: regular, independent appraisal by **internal auditors** of accounting, financial and other operations, at best comparing actual results with plans.
4. **Personal observation**: also called **"managing by walking around."**

### Time-event network analysis
- **PERT** (Program Evaluation and Review Technique): a **probabilistic** model that allows for uncertainty using **three time estimates**: optimistic, pessimistic and most likely.
- **CPM** (Critical Path Method): a **deterministic** model that does not account for uncertainty in activity times.
- Both are used for **planning and controlling projects**.
- **Gantt chart**: lists activities on the left against a time scale on top; each activity is a **bar** whose position and length show its start, duration and end, so progress can be seen at a glance.

## overall
### Why overall control?
Most controls cover a **part** of the enterprise (policies, selection, training). **Overall controls** measure **total accomplishment**. Three reasons:
1. Overall planning needs matching overall control.
2. **Decentralization** (especially product or territorial divisions) creates semi-independent units that need overall control to avoid chaos.
3. Overall controls are **generally financial** and measure a manager's **total** effort.

### Three overall control devices
**1. Budget summaries and reports**: a résumé of all individual budgets showing sales, costs, profits, capital use and ROI together, so top management sees how the **whole company** is meeting objectives. The budgets must portray plans accurately; attention should go to **important variations**, not minor ones. Very useful under decentralized authority.

**2. Profit and loss (P&L) control**: uses the **income statement** for divisional and departmental control. If the whole firm exists to make profit, **each part should contribute**, so its expected profit becomes its standard. Each division details revenue and expenses (with a share of overhead) and calculates profit or loss. Works best in **product and territorial divisions** where managers have wide authority; rarely applied to central staff and service departments.
- *Limitations:* high cost of accounting and intra-company transfers; departments may compete unhelpfully; may be inadequate for overall performance; duplicated records.

**3. Return on investment (ROI)**: the ratio of **earnings to capital invested**. Profit is seen not as an absolute but as a **% return on capital employed**; the aim is to optimize returns on capital. ROI can improve through a higher profit-to-sales ratio **or** faster capital turnover.
- *Advantages:* locates weaknesses (e.g. rising inventory lowers the return); focuses attention on the core objective; effective with decentralized authority; allows comparison within the company and with other firms.
- *Limitations:* data and allocation problems; excessive preoccupation with financial factors; may overlook social and technical developments; can make capital investment for new ventures inflexible; doesn't say what a *reasonable* return is.

## preventive
### Two ways to act on unsatisfactory results
- **Direct control**: trace the cause back to the **persons responsible** and get them to correct their practices.
- **Preventive control**: **develop better managers** who apply management concepts and principles skilfully and think in systems terms, preventing poor results in the first place.

### Direct control
Standards for quantity, quality, time and cost are compared with plans; a **negative deviation** shows performance below standard and calls for direct control.

**Seven common causes of negative deviations** (assumptions behind direct control): (1) that the responsible person will correct things at the right time, (2) uncertainty, (3) lack of knowledge, experience or judgment, (4) that performance can be measured, (5) that personal responsibility exists, (6) that the time spent is warranted, (7) that mistakes can be discovered in time.

### Preventive control
**Principle:** *"the higher the quality of managers and their subordinates, the less will be the need for direct control."* It needs a wide understanding of managerial principles, functions, techniques and philosophy.

**Three assumptions:** qualified, experienced managers make minimum errors; management fundamentals can be used to measure performance; the application of those fundamentals can be evaluated.

**Advantages:**
1. **Psychological**: managers know what is expected and see the link between performance and measurement.
2. **Higher accuracy** in assigning personal responsibility.
3. **Encourages self-control**: managers correct themselves voluntarily.
4. **Lightens the managerial burden**: preventing problems takes less effort than fixing them.
