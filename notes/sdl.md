## quality
### Why quality matters
Open international markets mean several firms compete for every sale. Price matters, but **quality is often the major issue**, and poor quality is expensive for both producer and customer. Quality applies to **services** too (banking, health care, education), and is often most visible there.

### Definitions to remember
- **Feigenbaum**: *"The total composite product and service characteristics of engineering, manufacturing, marketing and maintenance through which the product and service in use meet the expectation of the customer."*
- **Juran**: (i) quality consists of **product features which meet customers' needs** and thereby provide satisfaction; (ii) quality consists of **freedom from deficiencies**.
- Other definitions from the unit: **fitness for purpose or use**; the totality of features that bear on the ability to satisfy **stated or implied needs**; aimed at **present and future** consumer needs; **conformance to requirements**; **affordable excellence**.
- **Customer's view**: fitness for use. **Manufacturer's view**: conformance to the specifications that lead to customer satisfaction.

### Key terms
- **Product**: the output of any process: goods, software and services.
- **Product feature**: a property a product has that is meant to meet customer needs.
- **Customer**: anyone **impacted** by the product or service (external or internal).
- **Grade**: a degree of satisfaction, sometimes called "quality of design"; products that meet needs to a superior degree are *higher grade*.
- **Degree of performance**: how a product scores against competitors of the same grade.
- **Degree of excellence**: value from functions, timely availability, aesthetics, performance and cost.

> *"Customer is king"*: the producer must observe quality in **design, conformance and performance**.

## qualitydimensions
### 1. Quality of design
Before producing, find out **what users demand** (surveys, market trends). These needs are quantified into **specifications**. Quality of design is about the **tightness of specifications** (a part with ±0.001 mm tolerance is a better design than one with ±0.01 mm) and whether the product performs consistently over its life.

Factors controlling quality of design:
- **(a) Type of customer in the market**: tastes and needs vary by location and income. *A 10-seater three-wheeler suits rural/semi-urban shared travel; a small auto suits city commuters.*
- **(b) Profit consideration** and **(c) the environment** in which the product will be used: *a bus body fails on hilly village tracks where a jeep works; lab appliances may fail in real factory conditions.*
- **(d) Special requirements**: greater strength, fatigue resistance or interchangeability need **tighter tolerances**; electronics are designed for **quick part replacement**.
- **Services**: design is judged by **convenience and flexibility**, e.g. medical aid at home, personalized insurance.

### 2. Quality of conformance
How well the **actual product matches the design** and specifications. It depends on factors such as the process, operators, materials and inspection, and must **control process variability** (seen and unseen causes), often with statistical techniques. An **inspection programme** with customer feedback keeps defectives minimal. In services, conformance means each service unit meets the standard and the provider **responds to feedback** (an airline passenger judges everything from booking to luggage).

### 3. Quality of performance
**How well the product performs in the customer's use.** It depends on **both** design and conformance: a great design badly made performs poorly, and perfect conformance can't rescue a wrong design. It shows up in the customer's experience and in **sales**.

> **Chain to remember:** Design (right product) → Conformance (made right) → Performance (works right for the user).

### Achieving and maintaining quality: everybody's business
**Marketing** (finds needs), **R&D/design** (sets specifications), **planning**, **purchasing** (buys materials of specified quality), **production** (trained operators make it to design), **inspection** (reduces defects and variability), **retail houses** (make it accessible and service it).

## qc
### What is quality control?
**Quality control** is *the process through which we measure actual quality performance, compare it with a standard and take corrective action if there is a deviation*: a systematic procedure for meeting quality goals.

**Feigenbaum's Total Quality Control**: *"an effective system for integrating the quality development, quality maintenance and quality improvement efforts of various groups in an organization to enable production at the most economical level and to achieve customer satisfaction."*

**Why QC is needed:** processes are subject to **variation** from materials, tools, machines, labour, working conditions and operator skill.

### Objectives of QC
1. **Improve income** by making the product more acceptable to customers (QC looks at the quality of **future** production).
2. **Reduce losses due to defects**: less sorting, rework, shutdowns, returns and scrap.
3. **Judge conformity** of the process to standards and act on deviations.
4. **Build loyal customers** in the long run, leading to "profit escalation".
5. **Associate value with the product** as customer needs change, through feedback and process modification.

The ultimate aim: customer satisfaction, goodwill, confidence and the maker's reputation. QC assumes **key quality characteristics can be defined and measured** (length of a part, % impurity); it can reduce defects to a few parts per million. Key characteristics can be **technological, psychological, time-oriented, contractual and ethical**.

### Inspection vs quality control
| Inspection | Quality control |
|---|---|
| **Finds and sorts** defectives already made | **Regulates** quality and reduces variation in **future** output |
| After the fact | Preventive, systematic |

**Quality policy**: the overall intention and direction of an organization about quality, formally expressed by top management.

## stakeholders
### Stakeholders and their expectations
**Stakeholders** are people **directly or indirectly affected** by an organization's operations, or who can **influence** its decisions: investors, consumers, employees, surveyors, consumer panels, management–labour negotiators.

Relations with stakeholders are set out in **codes of practice**, which usually begin by promising to act with **integrity, fairly and reasonably**, and to accept **social responsibility** to the community. These codes create an obligation to behave in certain ways toward each group.

Stakeholders can take part in developing plans and policies, support improvement initiatives and monitor performance.

## qa
### Quality assurance
**Quality assurance** is *the activity of providing the evidence needed to establish confidence that the quality function is being performed.* It gives the customer **proof that the product is fit for use**, and recognizes early warnings of trouble.

The type of evidence varies: vegetables in a village market are judged by direct inspection; packaged goods need **lab testing**; medicines and cosmetics carry **expiry dates**; long-life products state favourable conditions.

A manager can assure quality if they can answer: Is the product **safe**? Are we **complying with the law**? Is it **performing within the warranty** period? Is it **marketable**?

**Three forms of quality assurance:** (a) quality audit, (b) quality survey, (c) product audit.

### Quality audit
An **independent review** comparing some aspect of quality performance with a standard. It may examine field complaints and corrective action, tool and gauge control, design changes, whether manufacturing specs match design specs, and inspection procedures.

**Types of audit**
| Audit | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Adequacy (management) audit** | Does the documented system (quality manual, procedures) meet the standard's requirements? |
| **Compliance audit** | Is the documented system actually **followed** by the workforce? |
| **External audit** | Done by a customer/contract partner (adequacy and/or compliance) |
| **Extrinsic audit** | External audit by an **independent, often accredited third party** using a standard such as ISO 9000 |
| **Internal audit** | By the company's **own staff**, to assure management the system meets its objectives |

A good audit report is **balanced**: it reports what works as well as deficiencies. Audits can't show market standing, user feedback or cost-reduction opportunities. Tips: prioritize activities, define the subject matter with a checklist, switch resources once an area improves, and use reference standards (a Quality Assurance Manual).

### Quality survey
A broad evaluation of an organization's (often a supplier's) **quality capabilities and arrangements**.

## productaudit
### Product audit
An **independent evaluation of product quality** to determine **fitness for use and conformance to specification**, done **after inspection**. Purposes:
1. Estimate the quality level **delivered to customers**.
2. Evaluate the **effectiveness of inspection** decisions.
3. Provide information to improve outgoing quality and inspection.
4. Give **extra assurance** beyond routine inspection.

It may be done by a customer, internally as "final inspection", or externally. Its main task is **sampling** for defects (sample size by statistics for mass production; many characteristics for large units like cars).

### Stages for product auditing (Juran)
| Stage | Merits / demerits |
|---|---|
| After acceptance by inspectors | Most economical, but misses packing, shipping, storage and use |
| After packing, before shipment | Needs unpacking and repacking, but checks original packing |
| On receipt by dealers | Hard to administer at many locations, but shows shipping and storage effects |
| On receipt by users | Even harder, but adds dealer handling and shipment to user |
| Performance in service | **Ideal** but hardest; simplify with sampling |

### Scoring and inspection effectiveness
Defects are weighted by seriousness: **A critical (100)**, **B major (50)**, **C minor (10)**, **D incidental (1)**; demerits are summed into a score.

**Inspection effectiveness** = defects found by inspection ÷ (inspection defects + extra defects found by the audit) × 100.
- *Example:* inspection found 40, audit found 10 more → 40/50 × 100 = **80%**.
- *Practice:* inspection 18, audit 2 more → 18/20 × 100 = **90%**.

Results are compared with a stable **base period** (excluding abnormal events) or with **market quality**.

### Effects of quality
Quality affects **costs** (fewer deficiencies mean less waste, rework and complaints) and **income** (features customers value increase sales).

## tqm
### What is TQM?
**Total quality management** is the **total involvement of everyone** in the organization in achieving quality, through a **participative culture**. TQM rose in the early 1980s; **Deming** introduced statistical measures (1939–45) and showed operators could remove only **15%** of variation causes, with management accountable for **85%**. The Japanese took him seriously; the West later followed.

**The key idea: the customer chain.** Everyone, every department, is a **customer of and supplier to** someone else. The external customer is just the last link, so **quality becomes everyone's routine duty**.

**Deming's definition:** *"A culture in which there is total commitment to quality, and an attitude expressed by everybody's involvement in continuous improvement of products and services through innovative scientific methods."*

### Principle of objectives
Global competition demands four abilities: give customers what they want **on demand at lowest cost**; deliver **high quality and reliability**; **keep pace with change**; and **stay a step ahead** of future needs. TQM's objectives: monitor performance and satisfaction; identify needed improvements at the customer interface; deliver better products at lowest cost; agree on customer requirements; tailor output to demand.

**Two unique features:** (1) **continuous improvement and innovation** (fewer errors and waste, faster response, better productivity); (2) **quality methods applied to the whole organization**.

### Why TQM is popular
- **Customer satisfaction and market share**.
- **Cost reduction**: less energy, materials and inspection stoppages; prompt delivery, higher productivity, less repair and rework.
- **Conducive environment**: **fear is eliminated**, so people admit mistakes, feel respected and work for the organization's interest.
- **New definition of success**: not just profit and share but **morale, efficiency, fewer complaints, better industrial relations and a happier workplace**.

### Three axioms of TQM: Joiner's triangle
An equilateral triangle of three equally important cornerstones:
1. **Commitment**: to never-ending improvement and innovation, led by **senior management**, backed by action and resources, **not just slogans** (slogans alone cause frustration). Ways to motivate: proper conditions, education and training, communication, modern leadership, good materials and tools, job satisfaction and incentives.
2. **Scientific knowledge**: of proper tools and techniques for **technical change**; fact-based decisions.
3. **Involvement**: everyone as **one team** for **social change**, including suppliers.

## basictools
### The seven basic quality tools (as listed in the unit)
More than 40 quality tools exist; these seven are the most commonly used with TQM. They rely on **numerical data**.

1. **Cause-and-effect diagram** (**Ishikawa** or **fishbone**): identifies and displays **possible causes** of a problem. Major causes are the big bones; contributing causes are small bones.
2. **Flowchart**: a pictorial representation of **all steps of a process**. Symbols: **rectangle** = activity, **diamond** = decision, **rounded rectangle** = start/end (terminal), plus a document symbol. Helps uncover trouble spots.
3. **Pareto diagram**: ranks causes by contribution so the **vital few** are tackled first. *Rejected castings: blowholes 50%, hard spots 30%, scabs 10%, shifts 7%, cold shuts 4% → fix blowholes first.*
4. **Run chart**: monitors a process over **time** to see if the long-range average is changing; the simplest tool.
5. **Control chart**: a run chart with statistically set **upper and lower control limits** around the average. The process is **in control** when no points fall outside the limits.
   - Grand mean X̄ = (X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙ) / n
   - s = √[Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)² / n]
   - **UCL = X̄ + 3s**, **LCL = X̄ − 3s**
6. **Correlation chart (scatter)**: shows the relationship between two factors: **positive, none or negative**.
7. **Histogram**: a bar graph of a **frequency distribution**. *Example: 20 spelling scores from 35 to 50; range 15; 5 classes of width 3 (35–38, 38–41 …), with frequencies 1, 2, 4, 5, 8.*

> **Memory:** *Causes, Steps, Priorities, Time, Limits, Relationship, Distribution.* "**C**an **F**rank **P**lay **R**ugby **C**ompetitively, **C**oach **H**arry?" (Cause-effect, Flowchart, Pareto, Run, Control, Correlation, Histogram).

## kaizen
### Kaizen
A Japanese word meaning **gradual, continuous improvement in small matters**, in every process, involving everyone. It starts from recognizing that every company has problems and can improve as long as perfection is lacking.
- Creates a culture where everyone can **freely admit problems**.
- **Urgency** about never-ending improvement; repetitiveness is seen as an obstacle.
- **Process over output**: mass inspection of output adds no value.

### Kaizen vs innovation
| Innovation | Kaizen |
|---|---|
| Short-term, **dramatic** improvement | **Slow, step-by-step** improvement |
| Large investment, little effort to maintain | Minimal investment, effort to maintain |
| **Result**-oriented | **Process**-oriented |

### Four kaizen practices
**1. Total quality control (TQC)** splits quality costs into three groups, arguing that investing in prevention cuts the others:
| Cost | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Failure: internal** | Defects found **before** shipping | Scrap, rework, failure analysis, 100% sorting |
| **Failure: external** | Defects found **after** shipping | Warranty charges, complaint adjustment, allowances |
| **Appraisal** | Checking conformance | Incoming inspection, final inspection and test, product audits, calibration |
| **Prevention** | Keeping failure and appraisal low | Quality planning, process control, audits, training, supplier evaluation |

**2. Kanban (just-in-time)**: cards that pull production. A **withdrawal kanban** shows how much the next process takes; a **production-ordering kanban** tells the previous process how much to make. Benefits: parts arrive **only when needed** (less waste, space, inventory), fewer non-value movements, better layout.

**3. Quality circles**: **informal, voluntary** groups (up to about **10 members** plus their supervisor as leader) that meet to **solve problems**; they also aim at members' educational development.

**4. Total Predictive Maintenance (TPM)** *(the unit's term)*: aims at zero breakdowns, gradually higher equipment effectiveness, better profits and a satisfying workplace. It uses existing resources to minimize waste through simple daily checks, tuning, adjustment, trouble-shooting and repair, avoiding new capital spending and breakdowns.

## deming
### Deming's 14 points for transformation of management
1. **Create constancy of purpose** toward improving products and services.
2. **Adopt the new philosophy**; old methods no longer work.
3. **Cease dependence on mass inspection**: build quality in, do it right the first time.
4. **End awarding business on price alone**; prefer a single, long-term, trusted supplier per item.
5. **Constantly improve** the system of production and service (reduce variability).
6. **Institute training on the job**.
7. **Institute leadership**: help people and machines do better; teach and counsel rather than judge.
8. **Drive out fear**; encourage two-way communication.
9. **Break down barriers** between departments and individuals.
10. **Eliminate slogans** and poster campaigns; workers can't fix system causes.
11. **Eliminate numerical goals and work standards**; replace them with competent leadership (easy targets breed complacency, unrealistic ones breed manipulation).
12. **End annual reviews / merit rating**; they destroy teamwork and focus on the short term.
13. **Institute a rigorous programme of education and self-improvement**.
14. **Top management's permanent commitment** to put everyone to work on the transformation.

> **Memory groups:** *Direction* (1–2) → *Better system* (3–5) → *Better people* (6–7) → *Remove obstacles* (8–12) → *Learn and commit* (13–14).

### The seven deadly sins
1. **Lack of constancy of purpose**
2. **Emphasis on short-term profits**
3. **Performance appraisal / merit rating** (builds fear, rivalry and politics; destroys teamwork and long-term planning)
4. **Job-hopping** (mobility of management, constant changes of leaders and direction)
5. **Use of visible figures only** (cutting research and training to show profit; ignoring unknown figures like lost customers)
6. **Excessive medical costs** (unsafe products, processes and workplaces, job stress)
7. **Excessive costs of liability**

### The PDCA (Deming) cycle
| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Plan** | Decide objectives, needed changes, performance measures, responsibilities, resources and contingencies |
| **Do** | Implement, usually small-scale, with everyone involved; training, customer survey, data collection, project teams |
| **Check** | Measure and observe the effects, analyse results against expectations |
| **Act** | Take corrective action; **standardize** and document improvements; then plan again |

> PDCA is a **loop**, not a line: each Act feeds the next Plan.

## juran
### Juran's contributions
**Joseph Juran** stressed: managing quality through planning, control and improvement; the **cost of quality**; the **Pareto principle**; and the **project-by-project approach**. He placed more quality responsibility on **middle management and quality professionals**, with top management providing guidance and policy. He said about **80%** of quality problems are the fault of **poor management**, not poor workmanship.

### The quality trilogy
1. **Quality planning**: identify **customers and their needs**; translate needs into a language everyone understands and develop the product; **optimize** the product and process; transfer the proven process to operations.
2. **Quality control**: **detect and correct adverse changes** from the set quality level (audits, control limits).
3. **Quality improvement**: raise performance to an **unprecedented level** (breakthrough).

### Breakthrough
A **breakthrough** is a **beneficial change from the status quo**, needed to overcome **chronic** problems (as opposed to firefighting **sporadic** ones). Juran's universal sequence:
1. **Proof of the need**: convince management, ideally in **money** terms (waste = money lost).
2. **Project identification**: turn chronic problems into projects, prioritized by **Pareto analysis**.
3. **Organization for improvement**: upper-manager guidance team; project-by-project improvement; analysis of each project.
4. **Diagnostic journey**: find causes by experiment and data. Errors are **inadvertent** (unintentional), **technical** (lack of knowledge) or **wilful** (intentional).
5. **Remedial action** (e.g. clear accountability, help workers spot defects, preventive maintenance, sampling instead of 100% inspection, Pareto to separate problems).
6. **Overcoming resistance to change**: e.g. let affected people **participate** in planning and executing the change.

## crosby
### Philip Crosby: "quality is free"
Crosby first popularized quality for top management, claiming **all quality improvements pay for themselves**. His main contributions: the **zero-defect concept** and a **14-point improvement programme**.

### Two meanings of zero defects
- **Defect-free product**: valuable as a **long-range** goal implying never-ending improvement; counter-productive as a **short-range** target.
- **Zero defects as a slogan**: a banner to motivate; works only if the drive is well conceived. Pressuring workers with a slogan **fails** however clever it is.

### The four absolutes of quality
| Absolute | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | Quality means **conformance to requirements, not elegance** |
| **System** | The system for causing quality is **prevention**, not appraisal |
| **Performance standard** | The only standard is **zero defects**: do it right the first time, on time, every time |
| **Measurement** | Quality is measured by the **price of non-conformance** (the cost of doing things wrong) |

### Crosby's 14 points
1. **Management commitment**
2. **Quality improvement team**
3. **Measurement**
4. **Cost of quality**
5. **Quality awareness**
6. **Corrective action**
7. **Zero-defects planning**
8. **Employee education** (the **6 Cs**: comprehension, commitment, competence, communication, correction, continuance)
9. **Zero Defects Day**
10. **Goal setting**
11. **Error-cause removal**
12. **Recognition**
13. **Quality council**
14. **Do it all over again**

### Steps in implementing a zero-defect programme
1. **Personal challenge**: employees voluntarily sign **pledge cards** for error-free work.
2. **Identification of error causes**: trace defect sources and fix them; employees report potential sources.
3. **Inspection**: train workers on quality points to monitor using a **ZD breakdown sheet** (operation sequence + critical factors).
4. **Motivation**: create a quality mindset by **attracting attention** (slogans such as "Up with quality, down with rejects", letters home, news articles) and **generating interest** (displays, workmanship awards, quality education).

## pokayoke
### Poka-yoke (mistake-proofing)
Pioneered by **Shigeo Shingo** of Japan to produce **zero-defect** products. Originally "fool-proofing", later **"mistake-proofing"**, meaning *to avoid inadvertent errors*. A poka-yoke device **either prevents a mistake or makes it obvious at a glance**; in production it detects the error and **stops the line** until the cause is removed, so the same error doesn't repeat.

**Example from the unit (pens):** put pen bodies in one dish and springs in another; count springs out for each pen. If a spring is left in the dish after assembly, a spring was missed, and it is fixed immediately at almost no cost.

**Benefits:** higher productivity, lower error rates, better morale (an alternative to demanding more worker diligence). **Limitation:** needs a certain level of quality already built into the system; on a line failing at many points it would stop production constantly.

### Concurrent engineering
Doing product design, process design and related activities **in parallel (in tandem)** instead of one after another. Benefits: **shorter development time**, longer product life, higher sales and revenue, more customers. Organizations progress from separate methodologies to coupling **project management and TQM**, and finally integrating all of them with concurrent engineering. It needs **superior project control**, since the main risks are market delays and costly rework from poor planning.

### Unit 2 key words
**Kaizen**: continuous improvement by removing non-value-adding activity. **Kanban**: an instruction card to produce. **Pareto principle**: most problems come from a few causes. **PDCA**: plan-do-check-act cycle. **TQC**: integrating quality development, maintenance and improvement across groups for economical production and customer satisfaction.

## tqmhouse
### The eight elements of TQM: the TQM house
| Part of the house | Elements |
|---|---|
| **Foundation** | **Ethics, integrity, trust** |
| **Bricks** | **Training, teamwork, leadership** |
| **Binding mortar** | **Communication** |
| **Roof** | **Recognition** |

### Foundation
- **Ethics**: the discipline of good and bad; **organizational ethics** (a business code of ethics) and **individual ethics** (personal right and wrong).
- **Integrity**: honesty, morals, values, fairness and keeping to facts; its opposite is **duplicity**, in which TQM cannot work.
- **Trust**: a by-product of integrity and ethics; enables full participation, **empowerment**, decisions at the right level, risk-taking for improvement, and customer satisfaction.

### Bricks
- **Training**: interpersonal and team skills, problem solving, decision making, job management, performance analysis, business economics, technical skills. Supervisors teach TQM philosophy.
- **Teamwork**: quicker, better, more permanent solutions. Three team types:
  - **Quality improvement (excellence) teams**: temporary, for recurring problems, **3–12 months**.
  - **Problem-solving teams**: temporary, **1 week–3 months**.
  - **Natural work teams**: small groups of skilled workers sharing tasks (employee involvement teams, self-managing teams, quality circles), **1–2 hours a week**.
- **Leadership**: *possibly the most important element*. An inspiring vision, clear strategic direction, values; TQM must be **introduced and led by top management**.

### Mortar: communication
Links all elements. Credible only if the receiver interprets it as the sender intended. Flows **downward, upward and sideways**, with suppliers and customers too.

### Roof: recognition
Recognize teams and individuals for suggestions and achievements, **best given immediately** after the action. It lifts self-esteem, productivity and quality.
- **Ways**: personal letters from top management, awards.
- **Places**: in front of departments, on performance boards, before top management.
- **Times**: staff meetings, annual award ceremonies.

> **Memory:** *EIT foundation, TTL bricks, C mortar, R roof.*

## ideatools
### The seven new QC (management) tools
The **basic seven** tools use **numerical** data and formulas. The **new seven** work on a predefined problem and organize **verbal** data into structure. The Japanese found them highly effective (the "second wave" of quality).

1. Affinity diagram 2. Tree diagram 3. Interrelationship diagram 4. Prioritization matrices 5. PDPC 6. Activity network diagram 7. Matrix diagram

### 1. Affinity diagram
Pinpoints the problem in a **chaotic situation** by gathering lots of related **verbal data** (ideas, opinions) and **grouping them by natural relationship (affinity)**.
**Steps:** state the problem → brainstorm and write each idea on a card → sort cards into groups of similar themes → write an affinity (header) statement for each group → draw the final diagram.

### 2. Tree diagram (systematic diagram, dendrogram)
Breaks a **broad goal into increasingly specific means** until reaching action items that can be implemented.
**Steps:** record the goal and constraints → generate **primary means** ("what must happen to achieve this?") → review both ways (objective ↔ means) → expand to secondary and further means → check from the bottom up: "will these items achieve the next level?"
*Advantage:* very systematic and logical.

### 3. Interrelationship diagram
Untangles **complex cause-and-effect** links to find **root causes and root effects**.
**Steps:** list the issues (from an affinity diagram or brainstorming) → arrange them in a **circle** around the problem → for each pair decide: no, weak or strong relationship → **draw an arrow from cause to effect** (solid = strong, dashed = weak, never two-headed) → **count the arrows**: most **outgoing** arrows = **root cause (driver)**; most **incoming** = **key effect (outcome)**.

## priority
### 4. Prioritization matrices
**Narrow the team's focus** before detailed planning. Use when root causes must be narrowed to the critical few, issues are complex and interrelated, and **resources are limited**. Needs **team consensus**.
**Steps:** agree the goal → list **criteria** (numerical, e.g. 4 of 5, or verbal, e.g. excellent to bad) → make an **L-shaped table** of options vs criteria → rate each (1 = doesn't meet criterion well … 5 = meets it extremely well) → total the scores; **highest total = top priority**.
*Example (LPG bottling plant):* high cost of a centralized compressed-air plant scored 5+4+4+4 = **17**, ranked first.

### 5. Process decision program chart (PDPC)
Maps **every conceivable event or contingency** in implementing a plan and prepares **countermeasures**.
**Steps (lawn-mower trial example):** set the objective → determine tasks (test wheel, blade, grass box) → list activities and **possible outcomes** (wheels work or skid; blades sharp or corroded) → identify **constraints** (mower ≤ 15 kg) → prepare a **contingency plan** for each failure (if blades corrode, find another material) → check for inconsistencies.
**Uses:** implementation plans for MBO and technology projects; showing decision-makers' intentions; forecasting contingencies from past data; countermeasures to reduce nonconformities.

## network
### 6. Activity network diagram (AND / PERT)
Finds the **most efficient path and a realistic schedule** by showing total time, task sequence, parallel tasks, **critical tasks** and **slack**.

**Terms**
- **Activity**: a part of the project shown by an **arrow**; takes time and resources.
- **Event**: the start or end of activities, shown by a **circle/node**; an instant of time.
- **Best (B)**, **worst (W)** and **most likely (M)** estimates.
- **Expected time: ET = (B + 4M + W) / 6**
- **Critical path**: the sequence of activities with the **tightest time constraint** on finishing.
- **Earliest start time**, slack, project (two or more activities with an aim, a budget and a schedule).

**Three construction rules:** (1) each activity is **one and only one arrow**; (2) each activity has **two distinct end nodes**; (3) for each activity ask what must **precede**, **follow** and run **concurrently**.

*Example:* preparing a budget for a new product: A forecast sales (10 days), B study market (7), C design (5, after A), D production schedule (3, after C), E cost estimate (2, after D), F set price (1, after B and E), G prepare budget (14, after E and F).

### 7. Matrix diagram
Shows the **presence and strength of relationships** between two (or more) sets of factors at their intersections. Types: **T, Y, X and C**.
*Restaurant example:* factors (food, staff, atmosphere) vs options (better food, better service, more décor budget, more staff, lower prices), each rated 1–3 for impact.

## tqmimplementation
### Is the organization ready?
Three scenarios:
1. A track record of **responding well to change**: ideal; TQM is easy.
2. **Performed well but can't improve** its systems; staff doubt change: needs a **management and leadership development** programme first (the most common case).
3. **Significant problems** (unstable funding, weak administration, poor skills or morale): TQM is **not appropriate** yet.

### Two approaches
- **Integrated management approach**: TQM fits the organization's own culture. Sub-choice one: the organization already works well and accepts innovation, so TQM becomes the "accepted advantage", implemented internally. Sub-choice two: TQM supplies missing processes and competencies (decision-making, measurement) through well-designed TQM initiatives.
- **Traditional management approach**: the **most common and the cause of most failures**. TQM is **forced onto existing culture** as a purely technical process by outside experts in a short burst of lectures. Behaviour changes for a while, but culture doesn't (real culture change takes **at least three years**), so TQM is abandoned when money runs out or a new fad arrives.

### Guidelines for implementing TQM
There is **no single right way**. Guidelines: top management **commitment** and a shared direction; **customer identification, surveys and feedback** reflected in quality and timeliness indicators; **quality teams** for specific problems; **organizational assessment and leadership development**; involve line personnel; **identify customers and their requirements**; **make a specific plan**.

## measurement
### Quality measurement system
**Measurement** assesses product characteristics or process parameters and gives **feedback** so the operation can adjust. Because TQM needs big investment, firms expect a return; three things block that return:

### 1. High cost of poor quality (COPQ)
**COPQ** is the cost of **non-value-added activities and waste**, typically **20–40%**. Cutting expenses (laying off a third of staff, selling assets) protects margins but barely touches COPQ. The firm that survives competition is the one that **cuts COPQ**: hard in the short term, but the only route to long-term profit.

### 2. Improper time management
People say they lack time. A **Pareto diagram** of time use shows most time spent on **firefighting**, paperwork, meetings and travel, with little on **fire prevention**. Proper management shifts time **to prevention**.

### 3. Lack of score keeping
Everyone talks about quality but **no one keeps score**. Managers should plot measures like **defects per unit (dpu)** or COPQ over time; a **scorecard** shows whether the team is winning or losing and stops quality work becoming mere routine.

### Unit 3 key words
**Affinity diagram**: groups verbal data by natural relationship. **Activity network**: nodes and arrows for scheduling. **COPQ**: cost of non-value-added activity and waste. **Interrelations diagram**: finds root causes and effects. **Matrix diagram**: rows and columns showing relationship strength. **PDPC**: maps contingencies in implementation.

## iso
### What is ISO 9000?
**ISO** is the **International Organization for Standardization**, a federation of national standards bodies of about **130 countries**, developing **voluntary** technical standards since **1947**. **ISO 9000** is about **quality management**: the part of management that sets and implements the **quality policy**.
- **Quality policy**: the overall quality intentions and direction formally expressed by top management.
- **QMS** covers procedures, tools, capability assessment and documentation.
- ISO 9000 is **generic**: it applies to any organization, large or small, goods or services.
- It is **not a product standard** (it concerns *how* work is done, not the result), **not mandatory** and **not a government regulation**, but it is an important **customer requirement**.

### History
Introduced in **1987**, adopted quickly and went global (90+ countries, including India). Revised in **1994**. Originally six standards:
| Standard | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **ISO 8402** | Vocabulary (quality terms) |
| **ISO 9000** | Guidelines for selecting and using the standards |
| **ISO 9001** | Most complete: **design/development, production, installation and servicing** |
| **ISO 9002** | **Production and installation** only (firms that don't design) |
| **ISO 9003** | **Final inspection and test** only, the least detailed |
| **ISO 9004** | Guidelines for **internal** quality management (technical, administrative, human factors) |

Criticism in the 1990s (firms could comply just by following their own manual) led to **ISO 9000:2000**: 9001, 9002 and 9003 merged into **ISO 9001:2000** (the certifiable standard); the 20 elements were reduced to four sections (management responsibility, resource management, product realization, and measurement, analysis and improvement).
- **ISO 9000:2000**: concepts and **vocabulary**.
- **ISO 9001:2000**: **requirements** for certification or contracts.
- **ISO 9004:2000**: **advisory** guidance to go beyond 9001.

### Why a management standard?
It ensures orderly, efficient work; a systematic approach so nothing is missed and everyone knows who does what, when, how, why and where; it is most needed in **large** organizations; and it reduces repeated customer inspections, cutting cost for both supplier and buyer.

### Benefits of ISO 9000
Confidence in own capability, consistency, timely delivery and fewer failures, plus: **improvement in business** (efficiency, less waste), **simplified purchasing decisions** (fewer vendor assessments), **qualifying for new customers** and global markets, **educational experience** and teamwork, **improved market image**, products accepted across countries, and **organizational renovation** (removing obsolete procedures, better calibration and traceability).

## isorequirements
### Requirements of ISO 9001:2000 (sections 4–8)
**1. Systematic requirements**
- **Establish the quality system**: identify and describe processes, implement the QMS, monitor and improve process performance.
- **Document the quality system**.

**2. Management requirements**
- **Quality policy**: easy to understand, relevant to the organization, with ambitious but achievable objectives.
- **Demonstrate commitment**: make sure people understand the policy; initiate and follow up implementation; accept no deviations or waste.
- **Quality planning**: measurable objectives for functional areas.
- **Control the quality system**: appoint a **management representative**; set up communication channels; hold **management reviews**.

**3. Resource requirements**: provide **competent, trained people**, suitable **infrastructure** and **work environment**.

**4. Realization requirements**
- **Realization planning**: identify needs and plan quality control during realization.
- **Customer processes**: identify requirements and review them **before accepting orders**; control customer communication.
- **Product development**: clear responsibilities, managed interfaces, recorded changes, verification.
- **Purchasing**: purchased items and suppliers meet requirements (delivery, after-sales); verify at own or supplier premises.
- **Operational activities**: control production and service provision.

**5. Remedial requirements**
- **Monitor and measure** quality.
- **Control non-conforming products**: identify them, prevent their delivery or use, correct them, prove corrected products meet requirements, keep records.
- **Make quality improvements**: use audits, policy, objectives and reviews; corrective actions must **prevent recurrence**.

## documentation
### Documentation for an ISO 9000 QMS
ISO 9001 requires an organization to *"establish, document, implement and maintain a QMS and continually improve its effectiveness."*

**Documents required**
- **Quality policy and objectives**
- **Quality manual**
- **Six documented procedures** (in this edition): **control of documents**, **control of records**, **internal audit**, **control of non-conforming product**, **corrective action**, **preventive action**
- Other documents: **specifications** (stating requirements), **quality plans** (which procedures and resources apply, by whom and when, to a project, product or contract), and documents for design, purchasing, work execution and inspection.

**Records** are documents that **state results achieved or provide evidence of activities performed**: management reviews; education, training, skills and experience; evidence that realization processes meet requirements, and so on.

> **Document vs record:** a *document* says what to do (information); a *record* proves it was done (evidence). Memory: *say what you do, do what you say, show that you did it.*

### Why documentation matters
- **Communication** of intent and consistent action (its extent depends on the products, processes, formality, communication skills and culture).
- **Evidence of conformity**: proof that what was planned was done.
- **Knowledge sharing**: preserving experience (e.g. a technical specification reused for a new design).
- A goal of the revision was a **simplified set of standards**.

## isoimplementation
### Steps to implement ISO 9000
1. **Exposure and training**: ISO 9000 familiarization for everyone (and certified vendors); share the plan, e.g. through an internal ISO 9000 booklet.
2. **Develop the draft quality manual**: study all existing quality systems and practices, collecting charts, records, reports and data sheets.
3. **Internal audit of existing systems and incorporation of ISO 9000**: task groups study the requirements, find gaps and rectify deficiencies.
4. **Certification by a third party**: send the manual to an external agency, answer its questionnaire, then host an on-site **audit** of premises and systems.

(The general path: install a task force → review operations → develop the manual → internal audit → rectify → third-party certification.)

### Three starting situations
- **Preparing to implement a QMS**: use the **process approach**: identify processes, understand their interactions, and document them as needed (process maps are optional).
- **Adapting an existing QMS** (e.g. certified to the 1994 version): make modifications for ISO 9001:2000.
- **Demonstrating conformity** of an established system.

## osha
### Why safety and health?
Many accidents come from **lack of safety awareness** and **unsafe conditions**; person-days lost to job-related disability are about **ten times** those lost to strikes. Workers face chemical, physical and biological risks (e.g. spray painters without masks, gloves or goggles). Long hours and poor rest harm health. Without safety effort, lost production, wages, medical costs and compensation come straight out of profit.

### OSHA
The **Occupational Safety and Health Act (1970, USA)** set up the **Occupational Safety and Health Administration** in the Department of Labor to:
- encourage employers and employees to **reduce hazards** and improve safety programmes;
- fund **research** on safety and health;
- set **"separate but dependent" responsibilities and rights** for employers and employees;
- keep a **reporting and record-keeping** system for injuries and illnesses;
- **train** more safety and health personnel;
- develop and **enforce mandatory standards**;
- approve state programmes.

### Types of OSHA standards
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Specification** | Exact requirements (e.g. sizes, locations and warning symbols of signs) |
| **Performance** | Minimum and maximum limits of achievement |
| **Particular (vertical)** | Specific to one **industry** or operation |
| **General (horizontal)** | Apply to **all workplaces**: environmental control, walking surfaces, exits, lighting |

### Benefits
Better community relations, less absenteeism, better morale, steadier work. **Employers** get help identifying and controlling hazards, and inspections focused on the worst hazards. **Employees** get participation in safety programmes and inspections, prompt response to serious hazards, and advice on their rights and responsibilities.

### Making safety rules work
Mass awareness campaigns often **fail to transfer** to real work. Rules work when employees **accept them** (not imposed from above), they **keep pace** with technology and schedules, they **don't cause needless inconvenience**, and they are **specific**, not vague.

### Assessing a safety and health programme
**Three methods:** (1) **checking documentation** of activities (e.g. self-inspection records: are the same hazards recurring?), (2) **interviewing employees at all levels** about knowledge and perceptions, (3) **reviewing site conditions**.

**Four key components assessed:**
1. **Leadership, participation and accountability**: a worksite policy putting safety above production; **visible top-management leadership** (approving inspection reports, regular time for safety concerns).
2. **Worksite analysis**.
3. **Hazard prevention and control**: inspection documents and corrective actions.
4. **Safety and health training**: regular training and periodic awareness checks.

## environment
### ISO 14000
A set of standards on how an organization's activities **affect the environment throughout the life of its products**, from production to **disposal**: pollution, waste, noise, resource depletion and energy use. Uniform standards avoid conflicting regional interpretations.

**It covers:** environmental **management systems**, environmental **auditing**, environmental **performance evaluation**, environmental **labelling** and **life-cycle assessment**.

**Key features**
- An **environmental policy fully supported by senior management** must exist, and be made known to **staff and the public**.
- The policy must commit to **compliance with environmental laws**, **continual improvement** and **prevention of pollution**.
- **Voluntary and flexible**; encourages **cost saving** by integrating environmental requirements into design and manufacturing; can give a **market advantage**.
- The system cycle: **planning → implementation and operation → checking and corrective action → management review** (the PDCA idea again).

### Unit 4 summary
ISO 9000 is the worldwide quality standard; implementing a QMS needs strong **documentation**; **health and safety** are integral to TQM, with OSHA guiding employers and workers; **ISO 14000** brings uniform good environmental practice.

**Key words:** *Document* = information plus its medium (paper, disc, photo). *Generic* = applicable to any organization. *Record* = a document stating results achieved, as evidence.
