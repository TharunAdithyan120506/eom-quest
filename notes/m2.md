## plans
### What is planning?
A **plan** is a **blueprint for goal achievement**: it specifies the resource allocations, schedules, tasks and other actions needed to achieve a purpose. Planning **bridges the gap between where we are and where we want to be**.

### Why plan?
- **Reduces uncertainty** and helps forecast the future
- **Provides direction** and a logical framework for the business
- Guides **resource allocation**
- **Minimizes impulsive and arbitrary decisions**
- Gives a **benchmark** to measure actual performance against
- Helps avoid mistakes and spot hidden opportunities

Planning answers the basic questions: **What** must be done? What are the **alternative routes**? **When** is the deadline? **Where** will it be done? **Who** is responsible? **How** will it be done, and **how much** time, energy and resources does it need?

### The eight types of plans (broad → narrow)
1. **Purpose / mission / vision**: the **basic function or task** of the enterprise. *Oil company: search for, produce, refine and market petroleum. DuPont: "Better things through chemistry."*
2. **Objectives / goals**: the **important ends** toward which activities are directed. **Verifiable** objectives make it possible to measure effectiveness and efficiency.
3. **Strategy**: a complete plan for achieving objectives with **three dimensions**: setting long-term objectives, selecting a course of action, and allocating resources. Usually made by **top management**.
4. **Policy**: **general statements or understandings that guide thinking** in decision-making. They define an *area* within which decisions are made, leave **room for discretion**, and are sometimes only **implied** by managers' actions. *Examples: hiring university-trained engineers, encouraging employee suggestions, promoting from within, competitive pricing, buying from the lowest of three qualified bidders.*
5. **Procedure**: a **step-wise, chronological guide** to routine activities. *A college admission: fill the form → collect documents → sort applications. A vacation policy is carried out by a procedure that schedules leave without disrupting work.*
6. **Rule**: a **very specific statement requiring an action or non-action**, with **no flexibility**. The **simplest** type of plan; guides action **without a time sequence**; may or may not be part of a procedure. *No-smoking zone; no-phone zone.*
7. **Program**: a **complex** of goals, policies, procedures, rules, task assignments, steps and resources needed for a course of action, **usually supported by budgets**.
8. **Budget**: a statement of expected results **in numerical terms** (a "numberized program"): labour hours, units, money, machine hours. Types: **variable/flexible** (varies with output), **program** budgets (each department identifies goals), **zero-based** (a combination of variable and program budgets).

### Telling them apart (the classic exam trap)
| | Policy | Procedure | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it does | Guides **thinking** | Guides **action step by step** | Requires/forbids **one action** |
| Discretion | Yes | Little | **None** |
| Time sequence | No | **Yes (chronological)** | No |

> **Trick:** "*Generally* we promote from within" = policy. "*First* fill this, *then* submit that" = procedure. "*Never* / *must*" = rule. A *number* target = objective or budget.

## plantypes
### Classifying plans by perspective
The same plan can be described in several ways. These classifications are **not mutually exclusive**: one plan can be strategic, long-term, directional and standing all at once.

**1. Content: strategic, tactical, operational**
- **Strategic plans** (upper managers) apply to the **whole organization**, set overall objectives and position it in its environment. Broad, less specific, five or more years.
- **Tactical plans** (lower managers) specify **how** overall objectives will be achieved.
- **Operational plans** are limited in scope and measured **daily, weekly or monthly**.

**2. Breadth: strategic vs tactical**
| Strategic | Tactical / operational |
|---|---|
| Whole organization | Specific parts |
| Sets overall objectives, positions the firm | Derived from strategic objectives; give the details |
| Basis for tactical plans | Updated continuously |
| Long periods, less detail | Shorter periods |

**3. Time frame: long-term vs short-term**
- **Long-term**: beyond five years; what the organization wants to become; high uncertainty; focus of higher levels. *Sales, brand awareness, public reputation, government plans.*
- **Short-term**: up to one year; basis for day-to-day operations; answers "*are we doing things right?*"; must fit within long-range plans. *Communication, networking, daily routines, staff training.*

**4. Specificity: specific vs directional**
- **Specific**: clearly defined, no room for misinterpretation; *what, when, where, how much and by whom* (process focus). *A lesson plan.*
- **Directional**: flexible general guidelines, *"go from here to there"* (outcome focus).

**5. Frequency of use: single-use vs standing**
- **Single-use**: for a particular, unique situation. *A one-day sale advertisement.*
- **Standing**: ongoing guidance for repeated actions. *A customer-satisfaction policy.*

## planningsteps
### The eight steps in planning
1. **Being aware of opportunities**: look at the market, competition, customer needs, and our strengths and weaknesses.
2. **Setting objectives**: where we want to be, what we want to accomplish and **when**.
3. **Considering planning premises**: in what environment (internal or external) will the plan operate?
4. **Identifying alternatives**: what are the most promising ways to reach the objectives?
5. **Comparing alternatives** in the light of the goals: which gives the best chance at lowest cost and highest profit?
6. **Choosing an alternative**: selecting the course of action.
7. **Formulating supporting (derivative) plans**: e.g. buying equipment and materials, hiring and training staff, developing a new product.
8. **Numberizing plans by budgets**: sales volume and price, operating expenses, capital expenditure.

> **Example (opening an EV charging chain):** spot rising EV sales (1) → aim for 50 stations in 2 years (2) → assume government subsidies continue (3) → options: own land, lease, partner with malls (4) → compare cost and reach (5) → choose mall partnerships (6) → plans to buy chargers, hire technicians (7) → capital and operating budgets (8).

## objectives
### What are objectives?
**Objectives** are the important ends toward which organizational and individual activities are directed.

### Hierarchy of objectives
Objectives form a hierarchy from the **purpose/mission** at the top, through overall enterprise objectives, down to division, department and individual objectives. Two ways to set them:
- **Top-down**: upper managers determine subordinates' objectives.
- **Bottom-up**: subordinates propose objectives that are then integrated upward.

### Strategies and policies
- **Strategy**: determining the mission and basic long-term objectives, then adopting courses of action and allocating resources to achieve them.
- **Policies**: general statements that guide managers' thinking in decision-making.

### Multiplicity of objectives
A business has **several objectives at every level** because it must satisfy many stakeholders: owners, employees, consumers, creditors, vendors and society. It sets separate objectives for each group.

### Quantitative vs qualitative objectives
| Quantitative | Qualitative |
|---|---|
| Expressed **numerically**, easy to measure | Not numerical, harder to measure |
| Increase annual revenue by 10%; cut production cost by 15% | Enhance customer experience; improve brand reputation |

## mbo
### What is MBO?
**Management by objectives** (also called *management by planning*) was popularized by **Peter Drucker** in *The Practice of Management* (1954). It is a **comprehensive managerial system** that integrates key managerial activities systematically, directed at achieving organizational and individual objectives effectively and efficiently. Objectives are **agreed by both management and employees**, set for every department, project and person, and then used to **monitor performance**.

MBO emphasizes **performance appraisal**, **short-term objectives and motivation**, and **long-term planning**.

### Process of MBO
1. **Setting preliminary objectives at the top**
2. **Clarifying organizational roles**
3. **Setting subordinates' objectives** (superior and subordinate agree, considering resources)
4. **Recycling objectives** (reviewing and adjusting them)

The detailed MBO cycle: enterprise objectives, planning premises and key result areas → superior's objectives → superior's preliminary recommendation for the subordinate → subordinate's own statement → **agreement** → subordinate's objectives → ongoing performance → **periodic review** with corrective help → final performance → **final review and appraisal**, which feeds new inputs into the next cycle.

### Key result areas (Drucker)
Areas where performance is **essential for the enterprise's success**: market standing, innovation, productivity, physical and financial resources, profitability, managerial performance and development, worker performance and attitude, public responsibility (plus service and quality).

### Verifiable vs non-verifiable objectives
| Non-verifiable | Verifiable |
|---|---|
| Make a reasonable profit | 12% return on investment by the end of this fiscal year |
| Improve communication | Two-page monthly newsletter from July 1, max 40 work hours to prepare |
| Improve productivity | Raise output 5% by December 31 at no extra cost, same quality |
| Install a computer system | Computerized control system by Dec 31, max 500 hours, under 10% downtime in the first 3 months |

> **Rule of thumb:** a verifiable objective answers *how much, by when, at what cost/quality*.

### Setting employee objectives
Identify key job tasks → set **specific, challenging** goals → let the employee **participate** → **prioritize** → build in **feedback** → **link rewards** to achievement.

### Objectives of MBO
Identify problems and opportunities; turn them into clear goals; build a system to achieve them; review the organization against objectives; set objectives for each job and unit; clarify policies; set up a review system.

### Benefits
Recognizes the need for planning; clear objectives and **accountability**; **participative** management; **job enrichment**; good feedback; efforts focused on goals; better performance at every level; **motivated people**.

### Weaknesses and problems
- Failure to **teach the MBO philosophy**; no **guidelines** for goal setters
- **Difficulty setting goals**; emphasis on **short-run** goals; danger of **inflexibility**
- Over-desire for verifiability: forcing numbers where they don't fit
- Change disrupts planning; low morale; lower-level goals take precedence; over-centralized authority limits participation; **paperwork**

## strategy
### Strategic planning
**Strategic planning** is the process of developing the strategy (direction) and action plan to achieve an organization's goals.

### The strategic planning process (Koontz model)
1. **Inputs**: people, capital, managerial and technical skills, plus the **goals of stakeholders** (employees, consumers, suppliers, stockholders, government, community).
2. **Enterprise profile** and **top management orientation** (values and vision).
3. **Purpose and major objectives**.
4. **External environment**: present and future **threats and opportunities**; **industry analysis**.
5. **Internal environment**: **strengths and weaknesses**.
6. **Development of alternative strategies**.
7. **Evaluation and strategic choice**.
8. **Consistency testing and contingency planning**.
9. **Medium and short-run planning**, then **implementation** (re-engineering structure, staffing, leadership, control).

### Three levels of strategy
| Level | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Corporate** | *What business are we in?* |
| **Business (competitive)** | *How do we compete?* |
| **Functional** | *How do we support the business-level strategy?* |

### Five strategy tools (related but distinct)
Critical question analysis · Blue Ocean strategy · SWOT & TOWS · Business portfolio (BCG) · Porter's generic model. Managers can use any one or a combination.

### Critical question analysis
Asks basic questions before choosing a strategy:
- **What is our business?** *Glass-bottle makers lost out by seeing themselves as glass-bottle makers instead of liquid-container makers, as plastic and metal replaced glass.*
- **Who are our customers?** What do they want? How much will they buy, at what price?
- Do we want to be a **product leader**? Develop our own new products?
- For marketing: where are customers and why do they buy? How do they buy? How should we sell? **What do we offer that competitors don't?**

## blueocean
### Red oceans vs blue oceans
Developed by **W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne**. Instead of fighting rivals in crowded, saturated markets (**red oceans**), firms create **blue oceans**: **untapped, uncontested market spaces** ripe for growth. It pursues **differentiation and low cost at the same time**, creating new demand and making **competition irrelevant**.

### Two principles
1. **Value innovation**: superior value through **differentiation + low cost**, redefining industry boundaries rather than just beating rivals.
2. **Creation of uncontested market space**.

### Advantages
Creates new demand and new markets; unique value propositions; avoids cut-throat competition; sustained profitability.

### Challenges
High risk of failure; significant resource investment; uncertainty of unexplored markets.

### Indian applications from the slides
- **Hindustan Unilever, Project Shakti (2001)**: direct selling of hygiene and sanitation products in rural India.
- **Airtel**: mobile banking services (2010).
- **Flipkart**: focused on the Indian consumer's needs with a unique shopping experience; later grocery delivery (2015).
- **Paytm**: created the mobile payments market in India when digital payments barely existed.

### The four actions framework (ERRC)
Sort the industry's competitive factors into four groups:
| Action | Question |
|---|---|
| **Eliminate** | Which factors the industry competes on should be dropped? |
| **Reduce** | Which should be brought **below** industry standard? |
| **Raise** | Which should be raised **above** industry standard to make rivals irrelevant? |
| **Create** | What new factors should be offered that the industry never offered? |

> **Memory:** *ERRC*: two cost-cutting moves (E, R) and two value-adding moves (R, C) together give low cost + differentiation.

## swot
### What is SWOT?
**Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats**, credited to **Albert Humphrey** (Stanford, 1960s–70s). A framework to organize information from a **situation analysis of the internal and external environment** and move from everyday problems to a fresh perspective.

### The four parts
| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** (controllable) | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| **External** (uncontrollable) | **Opportunities** | **Threats** |

- **Strengths**: internal positive attributes giving an advantage. *Abundant finance, well-known brand, economies of scale, lower costs, superior management, good marketing and distribution, committed employees.*
- **Weaknesses**: internal characteristics causing disadvantage; **controllable**, so they should be minimized. *Limited finance, weak R&D, narrow product line, limited distribution, higher costs, outdated technology, weak image, under-trained staff.*
- **Opportunities**: external conditions the firm can use to grow more profitably; from market, competition, industry/government or technology. *Rapid market growth, complacent rivals, changing tastes, new uses for the product, economic boom, deregulation.*
- **Threats**: external factors **beyond control** that endanger the business, worse when linked to weaknesses. *Foreign competitors, substitute products, declining product life cycle, new rival strategies, more regulation, recession.*

### Worked example: a car maker launching EVs
- **S**: eco-friendly, silent, simpler mechanism, low maintenance, energy saved by regenerative braking
- **W**: high price, recharge time, expensive battery replacement, lack of charging infrastructure
- **O**: huge untapped EV market, demand for green tech, rising fuel costs, lower taxes, government subsidies
- **T**: entry of competitors, rising electricity cost, hybrid vehicles

> **Sorting rule:** first ask *inside or outside the firm?*, then *helpful or harmful?* A competitor's action is always external.

## tows
### From diagnosis to strategy
**TOWS** links a firm's internal strengths and weaknesses with its external opportunities and threats to produce **four strategy types**. SWOT *lists* the factors; TOWS *combines* them into action.

| | Opportunities (O) | Threats (T) |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengths (S)** | **SO: Maxi-Maxi**. Use strengths to take advantage of opportunities (potentially the most successful) | **ST: Maxi-Mini**. Use strengths to cope with or avoid threats |
| **Weaknesses (W)** | **WO: Mini-Maxi**. Developmental strategy to overcome weaknesses by using opportunities | **WT: Mini-Mini**. Retrenchment, liquidation or joint venture to minimize both |

**Retrenchment** (a WT move): reducing the diversity or size of operations, withdrawing from markets or dropping products, to cut costs and become financially stable.

### Apple examples from the slides
- **SO**: strong brand + design skill + rising popularity of touchscreen smartphones → the **first iPhone**.
- **ST**: design strength and brand loyalty used to cope with the EU push for **Type-C** charging; loyal customers keep buying Apple's charger.
- **WO**: smaller product portfolio (weakness) overcome to enter the growing **wearables** market with the **Apple Watch (2015)**.
- **WT**: Apple TV device weak versus Roku/Fire Stick while streaming competition grew → launched **Apple TV+**.

### Time dynamics
Situations change, so TOWS should be redone over time (past, present, present + T1, present + T2).

## bcg
### The growth–share matrix
Developed by the **Boston Consulting Group** in the 1970s. Each **strategic business unit (SBU)** is plotted on two axes:
- **Relative market share** = SBU's sales this year ÷ leading competitor's sales this year (shows competitive strength; based on the experience curve and cost leadership)
- **Market growth rate** = industry sales this year − industry sales last year (shows market attractiveness)

### The four cells
| | High relative share | Low relative share |
|---|---|---|
| **High growth** | **Star** | **Question mark** |
| **Low growth** | **Cash cow** | **Dog** |

- **Question marks** (high growth, low share): most businesses start here; they absorb lots of cash; may become stars and later cash cows, or fall to dogs. Invest heavily **or** drop.
- **Stars** (high growth, high share): leaders; need heavy investment to keep share; generate and consume lots of cash. When growth slows they become cash cows. **Keep investing.**
- **Cash cows** (low growth, high share): the company's foundation, often yesterday's stars, in mature markets; generate **more cash than needed**; milk them with minimal investment to fund stars and question marks.
- **Dogs** (low growth, low share): **cash traps** in declining markets; minimize them; usually **divest**.

### Why use it?
To assess product profiles, cash demands, development cycles, and **resource allocation and divestment** decisions.

### Main steps
1. Divide the company into **SBUs**.
2. Assess each SBU's **relative market share** and **industry growth**.
3. **Classify** SBUs into the matrix.
4. Develop **strategic objectives** for each.

### Four strategies
**Build/grow** (question marks to stars) · **Hold/maintain** (stars, strong cows) · **Harvest** (cows, weak dogs/question marks) · **Divest** (dogs, hopeless question marks).

### Application: the "one size fits all" mistake
Giving every unit the same growth or return target means cash cows hit targets too easily and reinvest in mature businesses, dogs get money in hopeless turnarounds, and stars and question marks receive only mediocre funding, so they never become cash cows.

### Benefits and limitations
- **Benefits**: simple; quick screening of opportunities; shows how to use cash for future growth and profit.
- **Limitations**: only **two dimensions**; data for share and growth are hard to get; **high share ≠ always profit**, low-share businesses can be profitable; ignores **synergy** between units; growth isn't the only sign of attractiveness; "market" is not clearly defined; ignores small fast-growing competitors.

## porter
### Growth strategies
- **Direct expansion**: increasing size, revenue, operations or workforce.
- **Merger**: two companies of similar size combine to form a new company.
- **Acquisition**: a larger company buys a smaller one and absorbs its operations.

### Porter's generic competitive strategies
Long-run profitability depends on **sustainable competitive advantage**, which comes in two types: **low cost** or **differentiation**. Combined with the firm's **market scope** (broad or narrow), they give three generic strategies:

| | Low cost | Differentiation |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad target** | **Cost leadership** | **Differentiation** |
| **Narrow target** | **Cost focus** | **Differentiation focus** |

1. **Overall cost leadership**: gain advantage by having costs **below competitors**, watching costs in R&D, sales and service, so it can sell cheap and still profit. *Timex: simple, low-cost watches for the mass market.*
2. **Differentiation**: offer something **unique** in quality or service so customers pay more. *Rolex (handmade, rigorously tested), Porsche, Caterpillar (prompt service and spare parts).*
3. **Focus**: concentrate on a specific **region, product line or buyer group**, using either:
   - **Differentiation focus**: *Longines selling highly jewelled watches to wealthy women; Fisher-Price calculators with big bright buttons for parents of pre-schoolers.*
   - **Cost focus**: low cost within the narrow market.

### Stuck in the middle
A firm that tries every generic strategy but achieves none has **no competitive advantage** and is out-competed by cost leaders, differentiators and focusers. It may still profit only if the industry is very attractive or rivals are also stuck.

> **Two questions to classify any firm:** *Cheaper or distinctive?* and *Whole market or a niche?*
